F1

Fracture Description & Principles

A systematic, reproducible way to describe any fracture so referring clinicians can act on it without seeing the image.

ReportingFoundational

📋 The Standard Fracture Description Checklist

  1. Which bone, and which part (diaphysis, metaphysis, epiphysis, or a specific named region)
  2. Open vs. closed (clinical correlation — soft tissue gas or a defect suggests open injury)
  3. Fracture pattern: transverse, oblique, spiral, comminuted, segmental, greenstick, torus/buckle
  4. Displacement: the distal fragment's relationship to the proximal, as a percentage of shaft width and direction
  5. Angulation: direction the distal fragment points, in degrees
  6. Rotation: best assessed clinically, though cortical width mismatch between views can hint at it
  7. Shortening: overlap of fragments in long bones
  8. Intra-articular extension: implies risk of post-traumatic arthritis and can change management
  9. Joint involvement / dislocation
  10. Associated soft tissue findings: effusion, lipohaemarthrosis, swelling, foreign bodies, surgical emphysema

💡 Worked Example

"There is a transverse fracture through the distal radial metaphysis, with 20° dorsal angulation and 100% dorsal displacement of the distal fragment. The fracture does not extend into the radiocarpal joint. No associated ulnar styloid fracture is seen."

🔍 Two Views Minimum — Why It Matters

A fracture line lying parallel to the X-ray beam on one projection can be invisible on that view — the orthogonal (perpendicular) view is often what reveals it. Never call a joint or bone normal from a single view.

🩻 Fracture Patterns — Quick Differentiation

PatternMechanismKey Feature
TransverseDirect blow / bending forceFracture line perpendicular to long axis
ObliqueAngulation forceFracture line at an angle to long axis
SpiralTorsional/rotational forceHelical fracture line — consider NAI in children if history doesn't fit
ComminutedHigh-energy traumaMore than 2 fragments
SegmentalHigh-energy, two force pointsA separate middle segment entirely fractured off
GreenstickBending force in a childIncomplete fracture — one cortex broken, other intact/bowed
Torus/BuckleAxial loading in a childCortical bulging without a visible lucent fracture line
PathologicalFracture through abnormal boneLook for an underlying lesion at the fracture site

📝 Don't Forget — Report Checklist

  • State the bone and precise location clearly
  • Always comment on articular surface involvement
  • Always comment on alignment/displacement
  • Check one joint above and below any long bone fracture
  • Comment on soft tissues — effusion, gas, foreign body
  • If normal, state which views were reviewed rather than just "normal"
F2

Paediatric Trauma Principles

Children's bones behave differently to adult bone, and recognising non-accidental injury (NAI) is a critical responsibility.

NAI AwarenessGrowth Plates

🦴 Why Children's Bones Are Different

  • Thicker, more vascular periosteum — heals faster and often stays intact even when the cortex fails
  • Growth plates (physes) — the weakest point in a child's long bone, needing special classification (Salter-Harris)
  • Remodelling potential — significant angulation can remodel with growth, especially near a joint in a young child
  • More pliable/porous bone — allows bowing and incomplete fractures not seen in adults

📊 Salter-Harris Classification (Growth Plate Injuries)

TypeDescriptionGrowth Disturbance Risk
IFracture through the physis only (may be radiographically occult)Low
IIPhysis + metaphyseal fragment (most common)Low
IIIPhysis + epiphysis (intra-articular)Moderate
IVMetaphysis, physis, AND epiphysisHigh
VCrush injury to the physis (often occult initially)Highest

💡 Mnemonic: "SALTR" — Straight across (I), Above (II), Lower/beLow (III), Through (IV), Rammed/cRush (V)

🚨 Red Flags for Non-Accidental Injury (NAI)

  • Fracture pattern inconsistent with history or developmental stage
  • Multiple fractures at different stages of healing
  • Metaphyseal corner/bucket-handle fractures
  • Posterior rib fractures
  • Delayed presentation for a significant injury
  • Any injury in a pre-mobile infant warrants a high index of suspicion

💡 Key Points

  • Know your normal ossification centre order for the elbow (CRITOE)
  • A widened growth plate compared to the contralateral side can indicate a Salter-Harris I injury without a visible fracture line
  • Toddler's fracture: a subtle spiral tibial fracture in a young ambulant child, easily missed without oblique views

Skull Trauma & NAI

Skull fracture recognition on plain film and CT, distinguishing true fractures from normal suture variants, and paediatric skull assessment for suspected NAI.

NAICT Primary

💀 Adult Skull Fracture — Key Principles

CT has replaced plain skull radiographs for adult head trauma in most settings, but the same principles of fracture recognition apply.

Distinguishing Fracture from Suture

  • Sutures are symmetric, have a corrugated/interdigitating margin, and occur at fixed anatomical locations
  • Fractures are usually straighter, more lucent, can cross suture lines, and are typically asymmetric
  • Vascular grooves branch, taper gradually, and curve — fractures do not branch this way and tend to be more angular

⚠️ Common Pitfall

Normal accessory sutures and vascular channels are frequently mistaken for fractures. Look for the corrugated margin of a true suture and check for symmetry.

CT Findings to Actively Search For

  • Extradural Hematoma Biconvex, does not cross sutures
  • Subdural Hematoma Crescentic, can cross sutures
  • Depressed Fracture Inner table displaced inward past outer table
  • Pneumocephalus Intracranial gas — implies communication with an air-containing space

👶 Paediatric Skull — Suspected NAI

Skeletal survey protocols specifically include skull views because subtle sutural fractures can be more conspicuous on plain film, and this avoids additional CT dose when only survey is indicated.

Features Concerning for NAI

  • Complex or bilateral fractures
  • Fractures crossing suture lines (diastatic fractures)
  • Wide fracture width disproportionate to reported trauma
  • Occipital fractures (less common from simple accidental falls)
  • Any skull fracture in a pre-mobile infant without a clear, witnessed, high-energy mechanism

🚨 Key Point

A single, simple, linear, unilateral parietal fracture with a clear, consistent, witnessed low-level fall history is the pattern most compatible with accidental injury. Anything outside this pattern warrants safeguarding discussion.

Facial Trauma

Le Fort fractures, ZMC fractures, orbital blow-out fractures, and mandibular fractures.

CT PrimaryClassification-Heavy

😷 Le Fort Classification

TypeLevelKey Feature
Le Fort ITransverse, above the tooth apices"Floating palate"
Le Fort IIPyramidal — nasal bones, medial orbits, maxilla"Floating maxilla"
Le Fort IIICraniofacial dysjunction — nasofrontal, orbits, zygomatic arches"Floating face"

⚠️ Pitfall

True Le Fort patterns are often asymmetric or mixed. Describe the actual fracture lines and involved structures rather than forcing a pure classification.

🔺 ZMC (Tripod) Fracture

Fracture through four points: zygomaticofrontal suture, zygomatic arch, infraorbital rim, and zygomaticomaxillary buttress. Causes malar flattening clinically.

👁️ Orbital Blow-Out Fracture

Sudden rise in intraorbital pressure fractures the thinnest orbital walls — most commonly the floor.

  • Teardrop Sign Orbital fat/soft tissue herniating into the maxillary sinus
  • Muscle Entrapment Inferior rectus trapped — restricted upward gaze, surgical urgency in children ("white-eyed" trapdoor fracture)

🚨 Red Flag

A "white-eyed blow-out fracture" in a child — minimal external trauma signs but a trapped inferior rectus — is a surgical emergency requiring same-day repair.

🦷 Mandibular Fractures

The mandible is a ring structure — a fracture in one location often has a second fracture elsewhere in the ring. Always search the whole mandible, including the contralateral condyle.

Cervical Spine Trauma

Clinical clearance rules, systematic alignment assessment, and key fracture patterns.

Time-CriticalCT Primary

✅ Clinical Clearance: NEXUS Criteria

A patient can be clinically cleared only if ALL FIVE criteria are met:

  1. No midline cervical tenderness
  2. No focal neurological deficit
  3. Normal level of alertness
  4. No evidence of intoxication
  5. No painful, distracting injury

📐 Systematic Alignment Assessment (The Four Lines)

  1. Anterior vertebral body line
  2. Posterior vertebral body line — most important for canal compromise
  3. Spinolaminar line
  4. Posterior spinous process line

Any step-off or discontinuity suggests ligamentous injury or fracture, even without a directly visible fracture line.

🩻 Key Cervical Fracture Patterns

FractureLevelFeature
JeffersonC1Axial loading burst; check transverse ligament integrity
Hangman'sC2Bilateral pars fracture from hyperextension + axial load
OdontoidC2Type I = tip (stable); II = base (high non-union); III = into body
Clay-Shoveller'sC6-T1Spinous process avulsion — stable
Flexion TeardropLower cervicalHighly unstable, often with cord injury
Facet DislocationAny levelUnilateral "perched"; bilateral = severe, very unstable

🚨 Red Flags — Never Miss

  • Prevertebral soft tissue swelling (>7mm at C2, >21mm at C6 in adults)
  • Widened atlantodental interval (>3mm adults, >5mm children)
  • Bilateral facet dislocation — high spinal cord injury risk
  • Always image the craniocervical junction down to T1

T4

Thoracolumbar Spine Trauma

The three-column concept, common fracture morphologies, and stability assessment.

CT/MRIStability

📊 Denis Three-Column Concept

ColumnContains
AnteriorAnterior longitudinal ligament, anterior two-thirds of vertebral body/disc
MiddlePosterior one-third of body/disc, posterior longitudinal ligament
PosteriorPedicles, facet joints, lamina, spinous processes, posterior ligaments

Injury to two or more columns generally implies an unstable pattern likely requiring surgical stabilisation.

🩻 Common Fracture Morphologies

  • Compression fracture: Anterior column only — middle column intact — generally stable
  • Burst fracture: Anterior AND middle column — look for retropulsion into the canal
  • Chance fracture: Flexion-distraction through all three columns — strongly associated with intra-abdominal injury
  • Fracture-dislocation: All three columns + translational malalignment — highly unstable

⚠️ Compression vs. Burst — Key Distinguishing Point

On axial CT, check the posterior vertebral body wall. In compression fracture it remains intact; in burst fracture it is fractured and may retropulse into the canal — this distinction changes management.

💡 Key Points

  • Always measure and report the percentage of canal compromise when there is retropulsion
  • MRI is added when cord/cauda equina compromise is suspected, or to assess the posterior ligamentous complex
  • Multiple thoracolumbar fractures at different levels are common — scan the entire spine

Shoulder & Clavicle

Dislocation patterns, clavicle fracture classification, and acromioclavicular joint injuries.

Plain FilmDislocation-Heavy

💪 Shoulder Dislocation

TypeFrequencyKey Features
Anterior~95-97%Humeral head inferomedial to glenoid; Hill-Sachs and Bankart lesions common
Posterior~2-4%, easily missed"Light bulb sign" from internal rotation; needs axillary/scapular Y-view
Inferior (luxatio erecta)RareArm fixed in abduction; head displaced inferiorly

⚠️ Pitfall

Posterior dislocation is frequently missed on a single AP view. Obtain a second orthogonal view when clinically suspected, especially post-seizure.

🔑 Clavicle Fracture — Allman Classification

  • Group I (middle third): ~80% of clavicle fractures
  • Group II (lateral/distal third): Subclassified by relationship to coracoclavicular ligaments
  • Group III (medial third): Least common — consider associated intrathoracic injury with high-energy mechanisms

🔗 Acromioclavicular (AC) Joint Injury

Graded by the Rockwood classification (I-VI), based on degree of AC/coracoclavicular ligament disruption and direction of clavicular displacement.

💡 Key Point

Significant widening of coracoclavicular distance compared to the contralateral side suggests grade III or higher injury.

T6

Elbow (Paediatric & Adult)

Fat pad signs, the CRITOE ossification sequence, and the key fracture patterns unique to each age group.

Plain FilmEasily Missed

🔬 The Fat Pad Sign

  • Anterior fat pad: Normally a thin sliver — mild elevation can be normal
  • Posterior fat pad: Normally NOT visible — any visible posterior fat pad is abnormal
  • Sail Sign Markedly elevated triangular anterior fat pad = significant effusion, suggesting occult fracture

🚨 Key Point

A positive posterior fat pad sign should be reported as indicating a likely occult fracture and managed with immobilisation, even without a visible fracture line.

👶 Paediatric Elbow Ossification — CRITOE

Ossification Order (approximate ages)

CCapitellum (~1 year)
RRadial head (~3-5 years)
IInternal/medial epicondyle (~5-7 years)
TTrochlea (~7-10 years)
OOlecranon (~9-11 years)
EExternal/lateral epicondyle (~11-13 years)

⚠️ Classic Pitfall

The medial epicondyle can become entrapped within the joint after dislocation, mimicking the trochlear centre if the correct order isn't known.

🩻 Key Elbow Fractures by Age Group

FractureTypical AgeFeature
SupracondylarChildren (peak 5-7y)Anterior humeral line should intersect the middle third of the capitellum
Radial head/neckAdultsOften subtle — rely on fat pad sign
OlecranonAny ageUsually displaced by triceps pull; intra-articular
MonteggiaAny ageUlna fracture + radial head dislocation — check radiocapitellar alignment

💡 Radiocapitellar Line

A line through the radius should always pass through the capitellum on every view, at any age. If not, suspect radial head dislocation.

T7

Wrist & Forearm

Scaphoid fracture assessment, the eponymous distal radius fracture patterns, and carpal alignment.

Plain FilmHigh Missed-Injury Rate

🦴 Scaphoid Fracture

The most commonly fractured carpal bone — its largely retrograde blood supply means waist/proximal pole fractures risk avascular necrosis and non-union.

  • Dedicated scaphoid views are needed as standard wrist views can miss subtle fractures
  • A significant proportion of scaphoid fractures are occult on initial radiographs
  • Clinical snuffbox tenderness with a normal initial X-ray still warrants immobilisation and repeat imaging

🚨 Key Point

Treat a "clinical scaphoid fracture" as a fracture until proven otherwise — the consequences of a missed fracture are significant.

🩻 Eponymous Distal Radius Fractures

FractureDisplacementMechanism
CollesDorsal ("dinner fork" deformity)Fall onto outstretched, extended hand
SmithVolar (reverse of Colles)Fall onto a flexed wrist
BartonIntra-articular with carpal subluxationFall with wrist in fixed position
Chauffeur'sRadial styloid fractureDirect blow or avulsion

📐 Carpal Alignment — Gilula's Lines

Three smooth arcs across the carpal rows on the PA view. A break suggests carpal instability or dislocation even when individual bones look intact.

⚠️ Classic Pitfall — Perilunate/Lunate Dislocation

Frequently missed as individual bones can look nearly normal. Check the lateral view for the lunate's normal cup-shaped articulation with the radius, and check Gilula's lines.

T9

Pelvis & Acetabulum

Pelvic ring stability assessment, the Young-Burgess classification, and acetabular column fractures.

High EnergyCT Primary

💍 The Pelvic Ring Concept

The pelvis functions as a closed ring. A fracture in one part is often accompanied by a second injury elsewhere in the ring — always search the entire ring, including the sacrum and both sacroiliac joints.

📊 Young-Burgess Classification

PatternMechanismFeature
Lateral CompressionSide-impactMost common; sacral compression, overlapping rami fractures
Anteroposterior CompressionFront/back, "open book"Symphyseal widening/SI diastasis — vascular injury risk
Vertical ShearAxial forceVertical displacement of a hemipelvis — highly unstable

🚨 Key Point

Open-book injuries carry real risk of life-threatening haemorrhage from the greatly increased pelvic volume — a key reason for prompt pelvic binder application.

🦴 Acetabular Fractures — Column Concept

Conceptualised as anterior and posterior columns forming an inverted Y. Judet views or CT with 3D reconstruction assess which column(s) are involved.

  • Posterior wall fracture: Most common type, often with posterior hip dislocation
  • Both-column fracture: Articular surface separated from the intact ilium — "spur sign" on CT

📝 Report Checklist

  • Assess both sacroiliac joints
  • Measure symphyseal width
  • Check sacral foramina symmetry
  • Comment on any free pelvic fluid on CT
  • Look for active contrast extravasation — an IR priority finding

Hip & Proximal Femur

Femoral neck fracture classification, alignment lines, and hip dislocation patterns.

Plain FilmAVN Risk

📊 Garden Classification

GradeDescriptionAVN Risk
IIncomplete/impactedLow
IIComplete, undisplacedLow-moderate
IIIComplete, partially displacedHigh
IVComplete, fully displacedHighest

⚠️ Why AVN Risk Matters

The femoral head's largely retrograde blood supply is disrupted by displacement — this is why displaced fractures in older patients are typically treated with arthroplasty rather than fixation.

📐 Shenton's Line

A smooth curve along the inferior pubic ramus and femoral neck. Disruption suggests fracture or dislocation even when a fracture line is subtle.

🦴 Hip Dislocation

TypeFrequencyFeature
Posterior~90%Head superolateral to acetabulum; associated with posterior wall fractures and sciatic nerve injury
Anterior~10%Head inferomedial; associated with femoral head impaction fractures

🚨 Key Point

Hip dislocation is a time-critical orthopaedic emergency — AVN risk rises with time to reduction. Communicate promptly.

T11

Knee

The Segond fracture as an indirect sign, tibial plateau assessment, and lipohaemarthrosis significance.

Plain Film + CTIndirect Signs

💧 Joint Effusion & Lipohaemarthrosis

On a horizontal-beam lateral knee radiograph, a fat-fluid level in the suprapatellar recess indicates an intra-articular fracture communicating with the marrow cavity, even without a visible fracture line.

🚨 Key Point

A lipohaemarthrosis should prompt a careful search for a subtle tibial plateau fracture; if none is seen, consider CT.

🔑 The Segond Fracture

A small curvilinear avulsion off the lateral tibial plateau from the lateral capsular ligament — despite its small size, strongly associated with concurrent ACL tear.

💡 Key Point

Recognising a Segond fracture should prompt a specific mention that ACL injury is likely and MRI should be considered.

🩻 Tibial Plateau Fractures

Classified by the Schatzker system (I-VI). Lateral plateau fractures are more common than medial due to physiological valgus alignment.

🦴 Patellar Dislocation

Almost always lateral. Look for an osteochondral fragment from the medial patellar facet or lateral femoral condyle, sheared off as the patella relocates.

Ankle & Hindfoot

The Weber classification, Ottawa ankle rules, the Maisonneuve fracture trap, and calcaneal fracture assessment.

Plain FilmClassic Pitfall

✅ Ottawa Ankle Rules

Ankle X-ray indicated if pain in the malleolar zone AND any of: bone tenderness at the posterior edge/tip of either malleolus, or inability to bear weight both immediately and in the ED.

📊 Weber Classification

TypeLevelStability
Weber ABelow the syndesmosisUsually stable
Weber BAt the syndesmosisVariable
Weber CAbove the syndesmosisUsually unstable

🚨 The Maisonneuve Fracture

A proximal fibular fracture with syndesmosis disruption. The ankle film can look deceptively normal because the fibular fracture is outside the standard ankle field. Any medial malleolar fracture or deltoid injury without an obvious distal fibular fracture warrants proximal fibula imaging.

🦴 Calcaneal Fracture — Böhler's Angle

Usually from axial loading (fall from height) — check the contralateral calcaneus and spine, as bilateral calcaneal fractures and concurrent thoracolumbar fractures are a recognised association.

Midfoot & Forefoot

The Lisfranc injury and its subtle alignment clues, plus the Jones fracture distinction.

Plain FilmFrequently Missed

⚠️ Lisfranc Injury

Disruption of the tarsometatarsal joint complex — notoriously subtle on non-weight-bearing radiographs.

  • Fleck Sign Avulsion fragment between the 1st/2nd metatarsal bases
  • Alignment check: Medial border of the 2nd MT base should align with the medial border of the middle cuneiform

🚨 Key Point

If clinical suspicion is high but non-weight-bearing X-rays look normal, weight-bearing views or CT should be requested.

🦴 5th Metatarsal Base Fractures

FractureLocationHealing
Pseudo-Jones (Avulsion)TuberosityGenerally heals well
Jones fractureMetaphyseal-diaphyseal junctionHigher non-union risk — watershed blood supply

T14

Chest Trauma (Plain Film Focus)

Systematic plain film assessment for rib fractures, pneumothorax, and haemothorax in the trauma setting.

CXRTime-Critical

🫁 Systematic Trauma CXR Checklist

  1. Airway — tracheal position
  2. Breathing — pneumothorax, haemothorax, contusion, surgical emphysema
  3. Circulation — mediastinal width, cardiac silhouette
  4. Bones — every rib, both clavicles, both scapulae, visible spine
  5. Soft tissues and lines/tubes

🦴 Rib Fractures

  • Ribs 1-3: Implies high-energy trauma — assess for great vessel/brachial plexus injury
  • Ribs 4-9: Most common location for simple fractures
  • Ribs 10-12: Assess for splenic (left) or hepatic (right) injury
  • Flail chest: Three or more consecutive ribs fractured in two or more places

💨 Pneumothorax on Supine Trauma Film

Supine films are insensitive because free air rises anteriorly rather than to the apex.

  • Deep Sulcus Sign Abnormally deep, lucent costophrenic angle — the key supine sign
  • CT is far more sensitive and often used in major trauma protocols

🚨 Tension Pneumothorax — Clinical Diagnosis First

Requires immediate needle decompression — treatment should never be delayed for imaging confirmation in an unstable patient.

Abdominal Trauma (Plain Film)

The role and limits of the AXR in trauma, and recognising free intraperitoneal air.

AXRLimited Sensitivity

📋 The AXR in Trauma — Know Its Limitations

CT has largely superseded plain film for solid organ and bowel injury assessment. Understanding classic plain film free-air signs remains a core skill.

🔍 Free Intraperitoneal Air — Plain Film Signs

  • Erect CXR Best single view — even a small volume can be seen as a lucent crescent beneath the diaphragm
  • Rigler's Sign Gas outlining both walls of a bowel loop
  • Football Sign Large volume free air outlining the entire peritoneal cavity
  • Falciform Ligament Sign Free air outlining the falciform ligament

⚠️ Pitfall

Don't mistake a normal gastric air bubble for free air — free air is a thin, sharp crescent directly beneath the diaphragm.

T16

Foreign Bodies — Penetrating & Swallowed

Radiographic assessment for both categories, and the foreign bodies that demand urgent action.

Plain FilmSome Are Emergencies

🔩 Penetrating Foreign Bodies

Metal and glass are usually radiopaque. Wood/some plastics can be radiolucent — clinical history matters, and ultrasound is a good next step if suspicion remains high.

🍽️ Swallowed Foreign Bodies

Most pass spontaneously. The key job is identifying objects requiring urgent intervention.

🚨 Foreign Bodies That Are Emergencies

  • Button batteries in the oesophagus: True emergency — severe tissue necrosis within hours. "Double ring/halo" sign on frontal view, step-off on lateral
  • Multiple magnets: Can attract across bowel loops causing pressure necrosis or fistula — urgent removal even if asymptomatic
  • Sharp/elongated objects: Higher perforation risk at angulation points

Chest: Diffuse & Focal Disease

A systematic approach to non-trauma chest radiograph interpretation.

CXRPattern Recognition

📋 Systematic CXR Approach

  1. Technical adequacy: rotation, inspiration, penetration
  2. Lines and tubes: confirm appropriate positioning
  3. Soft tissues and bones
  4. Mediastinum and hila
  5. Lungs — compare side to side, zone by zone
  6. Pleura and costophrenic angles
  7. Diaphragm and below

🌫️ Diffuse Pulmonary Opacity — Pattern Approach

PatternKey FeatureCommon Causes
Airspace/alveolarFluffy, ill-defined, air bronchogramsOedema, pneumonia, haemorrhage, aspiration
Reticular interstitialFine net-like linear opacitiesPulmonary fibrosis, atypical infection
Nodular interstitialMultiple small discrete nodulesMiliary TB, metastases, sarcoidosis

🔍 Focal Pulmonary Lesion — Key Questions

  • Single or multiple? Multiple well-defined nodules suggest metastases
  • Calcification pattern? Central/popcorn favours benign; eccentric/stippled less reassuring
  • Cavitation? Thick, irregular walls favour malignancy or abscess
  • Growth compared to prior imaging? Stability is reassuring

⚠️ The Silhouette Sign

Loss of a normally sharp border indicates adjacent non-aerated lung — confirms an abnormality and helps localise it.

💡 Air Bronchogram

Air-filled bronchi against opacified alveoli confirms airspace disease rather than a mass or collapse.

Abdomen: Systematic Approach

A structured plain film abdominal X-ray assessment, gas pattern recognition, and common calcifications.

AXRSystematic

📋 Systematic AXR Checklist

  1. Bowel gas pattern: distribution, calibre, location
  2. Bowel wall: thickness, any pneumatosis
  3. Solid organs where visible
  4. Calcifications: renal tract, gallbladder, vascular, pancreatic
  5. Bones included on the film
  6. Soft tissues: psoas outlines

🌀 Small vs. Large Bowel Gas

FeatureSmall BowelLarge Bowel
LocationCentralPeripheral
Mucosal foldsValvulae conniventes — cross full lumenHaustra — do not cross full lumen
Normal calibreUp to ~3cmUp to ~6cm (caecum ~9cm)

💎 Common Abdominal Calcifications

  • Phlebolith: Round with lucent centre, pelvic — distinguish from ureteric calculus
  • Gallstones: Only ~10-20% radiopaque; ultrasound far more sensitive
  • Chronic pancreatitis: Coarse, speckled calcification
  • Appendicolith: Supportive but not diagnostic of appendicitis

🚨 Free Air — Always Check the Erect CXR

AXR alone is not ideal for free air detection — use erect CXR or CT if perforation is suspected.

CNS & Head/Neck Masses

Approach to intracranial ring-enhancing lesions and the anatomical spaces of the neck.

CT/MRIDifferential-Heavy

⭕ Ring-Enhancing Brain Lesion — Differential

Common

  • Metastasis (most common in adults)
  • Glioblastoma / high-grade glioma
  • Abscess
  • Resolving haematoma

Less Common but Important

  • Toxoplasmosis (immunocompromised)
  • Primary CNS lymphoma (immunocompromised)
  • Demyelinating lesion ("open ring" clue)
  • Radiation necrosis

💡 Key Distinguishing Clue

An abscess characteristically shows restricted diffusion centrally (bright DWI, dark ADC) from viscous pus, while necrotic tumours typically do not restrict in the same way.

🚨 Key Point

Gas within a deep neck collection or extension toward the mediastinum warrants urgent surgical/ENT involvement — distinguishing a drainable abscess from cellulitis changes management.

MSK: Bone Lesions & Arthritis

A framework for characterising a bone lesion, and pattern recognition for arthritis.

Plain FilmFramework-Based

🔬 Bone Lesion Analysis Framework

  1. Location: Which bone, and epiphysis/metaphysis/diaphysis
  2. Margin/zone of transition: Narrow = slow-growing; wide/ill-defined = aggressive
  3. Periosteal reaction: Solid/benign vs. aggressive (sunburst, onion-skin, Codman triangle)
  4. Matrix: Chondroid (rings/arcs) vs. osteoid (cloud-like) vs. none
  5. Solitary vs. multiple: Multiple shifts toward metastases/myeloma in adults

⚠️ Key Principle

Reassuring imaging features don't completely exclude malignancy — clinical context always matters.

🦴 Arthritis — Pattern Recognition

FeatureOsteoarthritisRheumatoidGout
Joint spaceAsymmetric narrowingSymmetric narrowingPreserved until late
ErosionsAbsentMarginal erosions"Punched-out" with sclerotic margins
Bone densityNormal/sclerosisPeriarticular osteopeniaNormal
DistributionWeight-bearing, DIP/PIPMCP/PIP/wrist, spares DIP1st MTP classically

💡 Key Point

Preserved mineralisation with punched-out, sclerotic-margin erosions distinguishes gout from RA.

S5

Paediatric Non-Trauma

Common developmental variants and classic non-trauma presentations.

Multi-ModalityDevelopment-Aware

👶 Developmental Dysplasia of the Hip (DDH)

Ultrasound is used in early infancy before ossification, using the Graf method. Plain film becomes more useful once the ossific nucleus develops (~4-6 months).

🦴 Slipped Capital Femoral Epiphysis (SCFE)

Typically overweight adolescents, sometimes with only knee pain (referred via obturator nerve).

  • Klein's Line Should intersect a portion of the epiphysis — in SCFE it passes above (fails to intersect)
  • Frog-leg lateral often shows the slip more clearly

🚨 Key Point

Always image both hips — SCFE has a meaningful rate of becoming bilateral.

🌀 Intussusception

Telescoping of bowel — most common cause of obstruction in infants/young children.

  • Ultrasound: Target/Doughnut Sign and Pseudokidney Sign
  • Most cases ileocolic and idiopathic in this age group
  • First-line treatment is typically image-guided enema reduction

🔴 Hypertrophic Pyloric Stenosis

Progressive, classically projectile, non-bilious vomiting around 3-8 weeks. Ultrasound assesses pyloric muscle thickness and canal length.

📊 BI-RADS Categories

CategoryMeaningAction

1NegativeRoutine screening

🔍 Mammographic Features of Concern

  • Mass margins: Circumscribed favours benign; spiculated favours malignancy
  • Calcifications: Coarse/popcorn favours benign; fine pleomorphic/linear-branching favours malignancy
  • Architectural distortion: Radiating lines without a mass — needs further workup
  • Developing asymmetry: New/increasing compared to prior imaging is more concerning

💡 Key Point

Comparison with prior imaging is a cornerstone of mammographic reporting.

⚖️ Differentials Library

Comparison-Focused

🦴 Fracture vs. Normal Variant

Suggests True Fracture

  • Straight, sharply lucent line
  • Crosses normal anatomical boundaries
  • Asymmetric
  • Associated soft tissue swelling/effusion

Suggests Normal Variant

  • Corrugated margin (suture)
  • Symmetric bilaterally
  • Fixed anatomical location
  • Branches gradually and tapers (vessel)

🧠 Ring-Enhancing Brain Lesion

Restricts Diffusion Centrally

  • Pyogenic abscess

Does NOT Typically Restrict

  • Necrotic metastasis
  • Necrotic glioblastoma
  • Resolving haematoma

🦴 Compression vs. Burst Vertebral Fracture

Compression

  • Anterior column only
  • Posterior wall intact
  • Generally stable

Burst

  • Anterior AND middle column
  • Posterior wall fractured — may retropulse
  • Potentially unstable

💪 Shoulder Checklist
