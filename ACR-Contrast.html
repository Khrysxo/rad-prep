The ACR Manual on Contrast Media is published as a web-based document by the ACR Committee on Drugs and Contrast Media. Its primary purpose: assist radiologists in recognizing and managing risks inherent in contrast media use.

Core Goals Before Any Contrast Administration
  • Assess patient risk vs potential benefit
  • Consider imaging alternatives providing equal/better diagnostic info
  • Confirm valid clinical indication
  • Informed consent may be waived for IV contrast per state law and institutional policy
Adverse Event Spectrum: Ranges from minor physiological disturbances to rare life-threatening events. Pre-arrangement of response planning, trained personnel, equipment, and medications is essential BEFORE any contrast-enhanced study.
Usage Note: "Low-osmolality" in this manual = both low-osmolality (~2× serum) AND iso-osmolality contrast media, unless otherwise specified. Manual focuses on iodinated contrast unless stated otherwise.

Risk Factors — Know What Matters

Greatest Risk: Prior allergic-like reaction to SAME class of CM
~5× increased risk for iodinated CM; ~8× for GBCM. Recurrence rate in non-premedicated high-risk patients: 10–35%.
Modest 2–3× Increase: Unrelated allergies (including shellfish, Betadine — NOT greater than other allergies), asthma
Do NOT premedicate or restrict CM solely for shellfish, Betadine, or asthma alone
No Cross-Reactivity Between Classes: Prior GBCM reaction ≠ increased iodinated CM risk (and vice versa) beyond any unrelated allergy

Premedication — Indications (May Consider)

Premedicate when: prior allergic-like OR unknown-type reaction to the SAME class of CM
Oral (12–13h) preferred for outpatients. IV accelerated (≥4–5h) when urgent.
Premedication Limitations
  • NOT confirmed to prevent moderate/severe reactions in high-risk patients
  • NNT to prevent 1 reaction (any severity): 69; 1 severe reaction: 569; 1 fatal: 50,000
  • Breakthrough reaction rate despite premedication: ~2.1%
  • 13h inpatient premedication: +25h LOS, +25h to CT, increased hospital-acquired infections
Breakthrough Reactions: ~81% are similar severity to index reaction. Prior mild reaction → <1% risk of severe breakthrough. Prior moderate/severe breakthrough = highest risk of repeat moderate/severe.

Premedication Regimens

ELECTIVE ORAL (12–13h): Option 1: Prednisone 50mg PO at 13h, 7h, 1h before + Diphenhydramine 50mg 1h before Option 2: Methylprednisolone 32mg PO at 12h and 2h before ± Diphenhydramine 50mg ACCELERATED IV (≥4–5h — HAS evidence): Option 1: Methylprednisolone 40mg IV OR Hydrocortisone 200mg IV q4h + Diphenhydramine 50mg IV 1h before Option 2 (MPred allergy): Dexamethasone 7.5mg IV q4h + Diphenhydramine 50mg IV 1h before EMERGENT (<1–4h — NO evidence of efficacy, last resort): Single dose MPred 40mg IV + Diphenhydramine 50mg IV 1h before
Switching CM agent within same class may reduce reaction risk. Combining agent switch + premedication has greatest protective effect. Many patients don't know which specific agent caused prior reaction — investigate via EMR if possible.
Skin Testing NOT Recommended: Not useful for predicting reactions; test injections may actually INCREASE risk of severe reactions
Bottom Line: Fasting NOT required for routine IV contrast administration
  • Modern nonionic LOCM and GBCM have very low nausea/vomiting rates vs old ionic HOCM
  • 2012 meta-analysis (2,001 patients, 13 studies): no aspiration pneumonia despite heterogeneous (including no) fasting practices
  • GBCM: 0.03% mild adverse events (nausea, vomiting, rash) in 158,439 injections
  • Randomized trial (Neeman et al 2020): no preventive effect of fasting on nausea, vomiting, or aspiration
Exception: Conscious sedation or anesthesia → follow ASA Preoperative Fasting Guidelines

Pre-procedure fasting has negative effects: scheduling limitations, hypoglycemic risk in diabetics, general discomfort. Benefits do not outweigh harms for routine IV contrast.

ONLY power inject through catheters CERTIFIED for power injection by manufacturer. Note flow-rate limit. 9.5–10F CVCs: up to 2.5 mL/sec shown safe. Small-bore peripheral-access CVCs: do NOT power inject unless manufacturer-rated. PICC migration reported after pressure injection.

Air Embolism

Treatment: 100% O₂ + LEFT lateral decubitus (left side DOWN — traps air in RV apex). Hyperbaric O₂ for severe. CPR if cardiac arrest.
CT appearance: air bubbles/air-fluid levels in intrathoracic veins, main pulmonary artery, or right ventricle.
Symptoms: dyspnea, cough, chest pain, pulmonary edema, tachycardia, hypotension, neurologic deficits.

Intra-Osseous (IO) Injection

Used in critically ill patients without IV access. Humeral placement preferred (higher flow rates vs tibial). High pressures needed due to intramedullary compartmental pressures. Case reports: up to 5 mL/sec, 300 PSI max. Require local anesthetic: lidocaine 40mg 2% (adult), 0.5 mg/kg (pediatric) before infusion.

Incidence: 0.1–1.2% of CT power injections. Most extravasations resolve without complication. Severe injury (compartment syndrome, skin ulceration/necrosis) occurs in <<1% of extravasations.

Risk Factors for Extravasation

  • Patient: uncommunicative, altered circulation in extremity (PVD, Raynaud's, prior radiation, venous thrombosis, axillary lymph node dissection)
  • Injection site: hand, foot, ankle (higher risk); deep brachial US-guided access (0.3% overall vs 3.6% with US-guided)
  • Contrast: higher viscosity (iopamidol 370 > iopamidol 300); 22-gauge catheter slightly higher rate than 20-gauge
Surgical Consult — Symptom-Based (NOT Volume-Based)
  • Severe or progressive pain/swelling
  • Decreased capillary refill
  • Change in sensation
  • Worsening active or passive ROM in elbow, wrist, or fingers
  • Skin ulceration or blistering
Most common severe injury: compartment syndrome (can develop hours after extravasation)

Treatment

What TO Do:
  • Elevate extremity above heart level
  • Cold compresses initially (empirical — vasoconstriction); warm compresses also acceptable
  • Observe, then discharge with clear instructions: seek care for worsening pain, paresthesia, decreased ROM, blistering/ulceration
  • Document all events; notify referring provider
What NOT To Do:
  • Do NOT routinely aspirate extravasated CM (rarely effective)
  • Do NOT inject corticosteroids or hyaluronidase (no proven benefit)
  • Do NOT use volume threshold alone to trigger surgical consult
  • Do NOT apply compression bandaging routinely
GBCM extravasation: ~1/6 as common as iodinated CM; lower tissue toxicity; small volumes → compartment syndrome extremely unlikely
Critical Distinction: Allergic-like reactions → consider future premedication. Physiologic reactions → NO premedication needed. Never conflate these categories.

Nearly all life-threatening reactions occur within the FIRST 20 MINUTES after injection

LOCM Incidence

  • Overall acute adverse events: 0.2–0.7%
  • Allergic-like: 0.6% (Wang et al, n=84,928)
  • Pediatric allergic-like: 0.18–0.46%
  • Severe reactions: ~0.04% (4/10,000)
  • Fatality: ~0.9/100,000 (meta-analysis Caro) or 1/170,000 (Katayama)

Pathogenesis

~90% involve direct histamine release from basophils/eosinophils. NOT classic IgE-mediated (IgE found in only ~4% of anaphylaxis cases). Multiple mechanisms: complement, kinin/contact system, nitric oxide, factor XII → bradykinin, pseudoantigen formation. Prior sensitization NOT required — explains first-exposure anaphylaxis.

Delayed Reactions (30 min – 1 week; peak 3h–2 days)

  • Incidence: 0.5–14%
  • Most common: cutaneous (maculopapular exanthem, urticaria)
  • Higher rate with iodixanol (iso-osmolar dimer) and IL-2 immunotherapy
  • Rare severe: Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SLE patients at higher risk), toxic epidermal necrolysis
  • Treatment: supportive (antihistamines, corticosteroids); premedication NOT specifically advocated for prior mild delayed reactions only
  • Recurrence ~25%; possible T-cell–mediated mechanism

Other Rare Delayed Events

  • Iodide "mumps" (sialoadenopathy) — more common with renal dysfunction
  • Acute polyarthropathy — more common with renal dysfunction
Key Principle: Viscosity is inversely related to temperature. Warming → reduced viscosity → improved flow, reduced injection pressure. Relationship is non-linear (turbulent flow through small-bore catheters — does not follow Poiseuille's law).

Davenport 2012 (Key Study): Discontinuing warming of iopamidol 370 → extravasation rate nearly tripled (0.27% → 0.87%, p=0.05). Hypersensitivity+extravasation combined rate also tripled. No effect seen for iopamidol 300. No clinical outcome difference (most extravasations are minor).
Nawras 2023 Meta-Analysis (307,329 injections): Warming of higher-viscosity CM → significantly lower hypersensitivity/physiologic reactions (OR 0.59, 95%CI 0.49–0.72). No significant benefit for lower-viscosity agents or for extravasation rates overall.

Logistics

Warming requires Joint Commission compliance: daily temperature log for each warmer + evidence of regular maintenance. Many institutions abandoned warming due to logistics burden and lack of clear clinical outcome benefit.

Gadolinium warming: One study showed warmed gadoxetic acid (Eovist) at 37°C reduced transient arterial phase respiratory artifact vs non-warmed (11.2% vs 21.1%, p=0.031 for arterial phase). Store GBCM at ~25°C; bring to room temperature before use.

Terminology — Critical Distinction

CA-AKI (Contrast-Associated AKI) = any AKI within 48h of contrast — CORRELATIVE (may or may not be caused by CM) CI-AKI (Contrast-Induced AKI) = AKI CAUSED by contrast — CAUSATIVE (subgroup of CA-AKI) CA-AKI ≠ CI-AKI. They are NOT interchangeable.
ACR Position: CI-AKI is real but RARE. Published studies heavily contaminated by bias and conflation. Newhouse et al: in >30,000 NON-contrast patients, >50% showed ≥25% SCr change and >40% showed ≥0.4 mg/dL change — pure physiologic fluctuation, not contrast effect.

KDIGO AKI Criteria

Within 48 hours of contrast exposure (any one criterion): 1. Absolute SCr increase ≥0.3 mg/dL (>26.4 µmol/L) 2. SCr increase ≥50% (≥1.5× baseline) 3. Urine output ≤0.5 mL/kg/h for ≥6 hours

IA vs IV route: Cardiac angiography (supra-renal IA) → higher CI-AKI incidence than IV. Three reasons: (1) direct abrupt renal delivery, (2) catheter dislodges atheroemboli, (3) higher renal dose concentration. IA data OVERESTIMATES IV CM nephrotoxicity risk.

Screening — When to Check Renal Function?

  • Personal history of renal disease (CKD, prior AKI, dialysis, kidney surgery/ablation, albuminuria)
  • Metformin use
  • Diabetes mellitus (optional)
  • Age alone and treated hypertension alone → high false positive rate; NOT reliable screening indicators
  • Acceptable interval: outpatients 30 days; shorter for inpatients

Prevention

IV Volume Expansion — MOST EFFECTIVE
0.9% normal saline (isotonic preferred over 0.45%); typical: 500 mL before + 3–12h after, or 1–3 mL/kg/h
Indicated: AKI or eGFR <30. Consider: eGFR 30–44 in high-risk. NOT needed: stable eGFR ≥30 or anuric dialysis patients.
NOT Recommended: N-acetylcysteine (no benefit vs placebo in PRESERVE trial), mannitol (no benefit), furosemide (may worsen), theophylline, fenoldopam. Bicarbonate: probably similar to NS but not preferred (needs compounding).
Contrast Choice: LOCM less nephrotoxic than HOCM. Iso-osmolar iodixanol: no clear advantage over LOCM for IV administration (meta-analysis 25 trials, n=>10,000). Do NOT reduce CM doses to mitigate CI-AKI — may cause nondiagnostic images.
Core Concept: Metformin does NOT increase risk of CI-AKI. Concern: IF contrast causes AKI in metformin patient → metformin accumulates → lactic acidosis risk (mortality ~50%; rate: 0–0.084/1,000 patient-years; no documented cases in properly selected patients).

ACR Management

CATEGORY I — eGFR ≥30, no AKI: → Continue metformin before AND after IV contrast → No need to reassess renal function post-contrast CATEGORY II — eGFR <30, AKI, OR arterial catheter with potential renal emboli: → Hold metformin at time of (or before) procedure → Withhold for 48 hours post-procedure → Restart only after renal function reassessed and confirmed normal GBCM at standard MRI doses (0.1–0.3 mmol/kg): → No need to hold metformin (GBCM not nephrotoxic at approved doses)
FDA vs ACR: FDA guidelines (as of 4/2016) are MORE RESTRICTIVE than ACR. ACR believes prevailing clinical evidence supports less stringent management. Document this discrepancy if ACR approach is used.

Common Metformin Drug Combinations

Glucophage (metformin alone), Glucovance (+glyburide), Metaglip (+glipizide), Janumet (+sitagliptin), Kombiglyze (+saxagliptin), ActoPlus Met (+pioglitazone), Jentadueto (+linagliptin), Prandimet (+repaglinide), Avandamet (+rosiglitazone).

Key Differences from Adults

  • Allergic-like reaction rates LOWER in children <10y and elderly >60y than middle-aged adults (Katayama, n=150,000+)
  • Osmolality more critical: neonates/infants susceptible to large fluid shifts → cardiac failure/pulmonary edema with hyperosmolar CM
  • Viscosity critical: small-gauge catheters + high-viscosity CM → catheter failure, vessel injury

Safe Injection in Neonates/Infants

  • Dose: 1.5–2 mL/kg typical (small volume → careful timing for CTA)
  • 24-gauge catheter: max ~1.5 mL/sec, 150 psi (Amaral study)
  • Small syringes generate HIGHER pressures than large syringes — caution
  • Consider hand injection when access is tenuous
  • Always verify CVC certified for power injection before use
Attention to site: Neonates, infants, developmentally delayed patients cannot verbalize symptoms of extravasation — monitor closely

Thyroid Monitoring (FDA 2022)

FDA recommends thyroid monitoring within 3 weeks of IV iodinated CM in newborns through 3 years.
Wolff-Chaikoff effect: supraphysiologic iodine → thyroid hormone suppression. Most escape in 1–2 weeks. Failure: true hypothyroidism. Vulnerable: premature infants, very low birth weight, CHD infants.
ACR Position: Risk very low <3 months; minimal-to-absent ≥3 months. Universal testing not strongly supported by literature.

Pediatric eGFR Calculation — CKiD U25 (not adult MDRD)

eGFR = (K × height in cm) / serum creatinine (mg/dL) K = age- and sex-dependent multiplier Best equation for ages 1–25 years (replaces older Bedside Schwartz for broader age range) Note: Newborn plasma creatinine reflects maternal creatinine for first ~3 weeks — unreliable marker of renal function in neonates.
Iodinated CM in neonates/infants: Generally safe regardless of serum creatinine/eGFR in absence of known kidney disease (Bedoya MA et al, Radiology 2017)

Ferumoxytol (Feraheme) — Off-Label MRI Use

FDA-approved: IV iron deficiency anemia in adults. Off-label: MRI contrast agent (growing use)
Advantages: Long blood pool half-life (14–21h), high T1/T2 relaxivity, no nephrotoxicity. Dose for MRI: 1–7.5 mg/kg (most 2–4 mg/kg); dilute 1:2–4 in NS; concentration ≤10 mg/mL. Bolus for angiography; slow infusion for lymph node/steady-state imaging.
FDA Boxed Warning (2015): 79 anaphylactic reactions, 18 fatalities in ~1.2 million therapeutic doses (2009–2015). 24% had multiple drug allergies; 50% reacted within 5 minutes. Contraindicated in prior IV iron hypersensitivity. Monitor BP/HR during and ≥30 min post-infusion.
MRI Interference: T1/T2/T2* shortening effects. Interferes with gadolinium enhancement if given within 72 hours. Effects persist: brain (days), liver (up to 6 months), bone marrow (up to 3 months). MPS (liver, spleen, marrow) accumulates iron → mimics hemosiderosis. No effect on CT, PET, US, nuclear medicine.

GI Contrast in Pediatrics

  • Barium sulfate: standard for fluoroscopic UGI/colon; avoid in perforation (peritonitis risk); aspiration of large volume can be fatal
  • Iodinated contrast: use for perforation concern, premature/VLBW neonates (prefer LOCM over HOCM); HOCM per os can cause dangerous fluid shifts + aspiration risk → chemical pneumonitis
  • Lumason (SonoVue): only US contrast FDA-approved for pediatric bladder (contrast-enhanced voiding urosonography)
  • Neutral oral contrast (enterography): Breeza preferred by pediatric patients over NeuLumEX for flavor

Barium Sulfate

  • Choice for fluoroscopy (superior mucosal detail, resistance to dilution)
  • Single contrast: optimal 60% w/v; double contrast colon: up to 250% w/v
  • CT: dilute barium used for bowel opacification (routine or targeted use)
  • NOT for CT/MR enterography (may obscure mural/mucosal abnormalities) → use neutral agents
Barium Complications:
  • Perforation → barium peritonitis: colonic leak (highest mortality, highest bacterial count), esophageal leak (mediastinitis), gastric/duodenal/small bowel (peritonitis)
  • Aspiration: ciliary action usually clears; large volume → obstructive pneumonia or death
  • Barium toxicity (rare): dissociation from BaSO₄ → soluble barium absorbed → severe hypokalemia → muscle weakness, respiratory arrest, cardiac arrhythmia, death. Tx: aggressive K+ replacement.
When to use water-soluble FIRST: Suspected perforation/leak/fistula. If water-soluble misses suspected perforation → then give barium (barium better detects small leaks).

Gadoxetate Disodium (Eovist/Primovist) — Transient Dyspnea

"Transient dyspnea" / breath-holding difficulty during arterial phase: 4–14% incidence
PHYSIOLOGIC reaction (not allergic-like) → corticosteroid prophylaxis NOT helpful
Risk factors: dose >10 mL (20 mL = 2× risk vs 10 mL); COPD (35–40% rate); prior episode (60% repeat rate vs 5% naïve)
Mitigation: ≤10 mL dose; avoid in COPD and prior-episode patients; multiple arterial phases with short temporal footprint

Gadolinium Retention / Deposition

T1 hyperintensity in globus pallidus and dentate nucleus after repeated linear GBCM doses. Macrocyclic agents: much less/no deposition. No confirmed adverse health effects from retention to date. Each IV GBCM administration: record identity and dose. Especially important in pediatric patients and those receiving multiple MRI studies over lifetime.

ACR-ASNR Position Statement

  • Use lowest effective dose; consider macrocyclic agents where appropriate
  • Benefit of GBCM administration in appropriate indications outweighs risk of deposition
  • Document identity and dose after each IV administration
  • Particular attention for patients receiving many GBCM-enhanced MRI studies over lifetime

Serum Calcium Interference

Some linear nonionic GBCMs (gadoversetamide, gadodiamide) interfere with the orthocresolphthalein colorimetric assay → falsely LOW serum calcium values. Not actual hypocalcemia. Only this one assay affected; gadopentetate and gadoteridol do NOT cause this interference. Clinical pitfall: do not treat "lab hypocalcemia" as real.

Sickle Cell Disease and GBCM

GBCM is safe in sickle cell disease at approved doses. No documented in vivo vaso-occlusive or hemolytic complications from IV GBCM in sickle cell patients. Small retrospective study with controls: no significantly increased risk. Do not withhold GBCM when clinically indicated.

Gadolinium Pregnancy Screening Statement (2023 Addition)

GBCAs cross placenta → fetal circulation (demonstrated in primates within 11 min).
Accumulate in amniotic fluid → fetal swallows → cycles → prolonged exposure → free Gd³⁺ dissociation risk.
2016 Canadian retrospective study: GBCM in pregnancy associated with increased risk of stillbirth/neonatal death and rheumatologic/inflammatory conditions in offspring (first-trimester exposure). Results have limitations; not independently confirmed.
ACR recommends: Standardized pregnancy screening before GBCM; use only when potential significant benefit outweighs unknown risk; document discussion with referring provider and patient; use Group II agent at lowest dose.
NSF: Fibrotic disease primarily of skin and subcutaneous tissues; also lungs, esophagus, heart, skeletal muscles. Rapidly progressive; sometimes fatal. GBCM exposure necessary for development (rare exceptions). Time from injection to onset: days to months (occasionally years).

At-Risk Patient Definition (for Group I and III Agents)

  • On dialysis (any form)
  • Severe/end-stage CKD — CKD Stage 4 or 5 (eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73m²) without dialysis
  • Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)
  • Estimated 1–7% NSF risk per Group I GBCM exposure in eGFR <30
  • 12–20% of NSF cases occurred in patients with AKI

Management by eGFR

eGFR ≥60 (CKD 1–2): Any GBCM safe; no special precautions eGFR 30–59 (CKD 3): NSF exceedingly rare; no special precautions needed eGFR <30 (CKD 4–5): Group I CONTRAINDICATED → Use Group II Dialysis (any type): Group I CONTRAINDICATED → Use Group II AKI: Avoid Group I → Use Group II if GBCM truly needed
Group II agents strongly preferred in ALL at-risk patients. Since restricting Group I use, new NSF cases have dramatically decreased or been eliminated. The risks of withholding contrast-enhanced imaging must be weighed against the very low NSF risk of Group II agents.
Multiple doses: For patients not at NSF risk: no contraindication if examination is necessary. For at-risk patients: use Group II agents. Do NOT initiate or alter dialysis schedule solely for GBCM administration.
Hepatic disease alone is NOT an independent NSF risk factor. In 335 NSF patients with liver disease, all but one also had severe renal insufficiency (eGFR <30). Liver disease without concurrent severe CKD/AKI does not independently predict NSF.

Screening Outpatients for NSF Risk (Group I and III)

Questionnaire-based risk factor screening (history of renal disease, dialysis, kidney transplant, single kidney, kidney surgery, known cancer involving kidneys, CKD/prior AKI, diabetes mellitus). If positive → obtain eGFR. Simply asking "do you have a kidney problem?" misses majority of patients with eGFR <30.

Inpatients: Obtain eGFR within 2 days before planned Group I or III GBCM. Assess for AKI (eGFR alone limited in AKI detection).

Postulated Mechanism

Gadolinium dissociates from chelate during prolonged renal clearance → free Gd³⁺ binds phosphate → insoluble precipitate deposits in tissues → fibrocyte activation → fibrosis. Linear agents (e.g., gadodiamide) less thermodynamically/kinetically stable than macrocyclic agents → more free Gd³⁺ release.

  • Majority of reactions: mild and physiologic (headache, warmth, flushing, nausea, altered taste)
  • Most severe reactions occur within 30 minutes
  • No known renal toxicity at approved doses
  • No adequate well-controlled studies in pregnant women — use only if clearly needed
Contraindications:
  • Intra-arterial injection
  • Prior hypersensitivity to microspheres
  • Unstable cardiopulmonary conditions (acute MI, unstable CHF) → increased serious reaction risk
  • Right-to-left shunt: REMOVED from contraindications in updated FDA labeling
  • Optison: contains human albumin (theoretical viral/prion risk; contraindicated with religious/ethical objections to human blood products)

Physics of Microbubbles

Microbubbles 1–4 µm diameter oscillate in resonance with diagnostic US frequency range (1–4 MHz). At high acoustic pressures, microbubbles rupture and dissolve, releasing inert gas eliminated via lungs. Shell composition and gas type determine stability, enhancement duration, and acoustic properties.

  • Crohn's disease: bowel wall vascularity assessment (off-label)

Iodinated CM in Pregnancy

IV iodinated LOCM in pregnancy: No evidence of mutagenic or teratogenic effects. Crosses placenta but clinically insignificant. No documented neonatal hypothyroidism from maternal IV iodinated CM.
ACR: Do NOT withhold iodinated CM when clinically indicated in pregnancy. No routine pregnancy screening required before iodinated CM use.

GBCM in Pregnancy

GBCAs: Administer ONLY when potential significant benefit outweighs unknown risk to fetus.
Required documentation (radiologist + referring physician agreement):
1. Information unavailable without IV contrast or alternative imaging
2. Information affects care during pregnancy
3. Not prudent to wait until post-delivery
Use Group II agent at lowest dose needed. Informed consent recommended.

Premedication in Pregnant Patients

Diphenhydramine: FDA Category B (safe). Prednisone/methylprednisolone: FDA Category C (traverse placenta; most metabolized by placenta; generally safe; small cleft lip risk with methylprednisolone before 10 weeks). Expert opinion: indicated premedication should NOT be withheld due to pregnancy.

Positioning for Hypotension in Pregnancy

Left lateral decubitus OR supine with leftward tilt (wedge) to relieve IVC compression by gravid uterus. If CPR needed: supine position with manual uterine displacement upward and to the left.

Breastfeeding — 2024 Evidence-Based Update

Both iodinated CM and GBCM: Breastfeeding does NOT need to be interrupted.
  • Iodinated CM in breast milk: <0.01% of maternal dose excreted; infant absorbs <1% of ingested
  • GBCM absorbed by infant: estimated <0.0004% of maternal dose
  • Both: clinically insignificant amounts reaching infant
  • ACR: inform patient and make shared decision
  • Strength of Evidence: Limited (but consistent across studies)
