Orientation

How to use this

Read the intent behind the structure once, then never think about it again.

Why this file exists separately

Your first suite covers the on-call textbook material — acute CT, emergency cross-sectional pathology, the classic call-night differentials. This one covers the other half of what gets asked: plain-film trauma by region, and systems-based pattern recognition on radiographs.

They deliberately do not share a storage key. You can run both, and your XP in one will not clobber the other.

The shape of every topic page

  1. Why it matters on call — the one-line reason this region shows up at 3am.
  2. What you're looking at — standard views and normal anatomy landmarks.
  3. Systematic search — the order to look in, so you stop free-styling.
  4. Injuries and patterns — the named entities, each with the finding that makes the call.
  5. Never miss — the red-boxed items. These are the ones with medico-legal teeth.
  6. Traps — normal variants and pseudo-lesions that fool people.
  7. Mini-quiz — five to ten questions drawn from that topic only.

Two difficulty levels

FY — first-year level. Recognition, naming, standard management triggers. If you can't answer these cold, read the topic again.

ADV — board level. Mechanism reasoning, classification systems, the second-order question after you've spotted the obvious finding.

On images

There are no embedded textbook figures here — those belong to their publishers. Each topic instead links to the matching Radiopaedia article, which has freely licensed case imaging. Open the link, scroll the case stack, then come back and do the quiz.

The diagrams that are drawn in-app are original SVG line schematics for things where geometry is the whole point: fat pads, cortical lines, Shenton's line, the Lisfranc alignment.

Tactics

Exam guide

How to answer, not what to know.

Structure every answer the same way

  1. Name the study, the projection, and any obvious technical limitation.
  2. State the main abnormality in one sentence, in the correct anatomical language.
  3. Give the relevant negatives — the associated injuries you specifically looked for and excluded.
  4. Give a diagnosis or a shortlist of two to three, ordered by likelihood.
  5. Say what happens next: the next imaging step, or who you call.

Steps 3 and 5 are where marks are won. Almost everyone gets step 2.

Common failure modes

  • Satisfaction of search. You find the obvious fracture and stop. Force yourself through the full search pattern anyway — second injuries are the whole point of the question.
  • Naming without describing. "Colles fracture" alone scores less than a clean description of a dorsally angulated, dorsally displaced distal radial fracture.
  • Forgetting the joint above and below. Especially in paired-bone forearm and lower-leg injuries.
  • Ignoring soft tissues. Fat pads, joint effusions, subcutaneous emphysema and prevertebral swelling frequently carry the diagnosis.
  • Not saying what you'd do. If a finding needs an urgent call, say so unprompted.

When you genuinely cannot see anything

Say what you have excluded, state the review areas you have specifically checked, and then commit to a next step: repeat films after an interval, cross-sectional imaging, or clinical correlation with a named question. "Normal" with a stated search pattern behind it is a defensible answer. Silence is not.

The single best habitBefore you speak, look once more at the edges of the film, the corners, and anything included incidentally. That is where the second finding lives.
