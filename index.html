Chapter 1 · Brain, Head & Neck

Intracranial haemorrhage & traumatic brain injury

📖 Requisites pp. 1–10FY + Board

Non-contrast CT is the workhorse for acute head injury. Acute blood is hyperdense (50–70 HU) because of clot retraction and haemoglobin protein concentration; density falls roughly 1.5 HU/day, so subacute blood becomes isodense to brain at 1–3 weeks and hypodense (near CSF) after that. Anaemic patients (Hb <8–10 g/dL) and coagulopathic patients can have acute blood that is isodense — a classic trap.

Venous EDH (from a dural sinus, e.g. at the vertex or posterior fossa) can cross sutures and is often slower to evolve; a vertex EDH is easily missed on axial images — look on coronal reformats. Posterior fossa EDH from a torn transverse sinus is a surgical emergency because of limited space and risk of brainstem compression.

Subdural collections: acute (hyperdense, days), subacute (isodense, 1–3 weeks — look for effacement of sulci, displaced grey–white interface, mass effect without a visible collection; contrast or MRI helps), chronic (hypodense, membranes, loculation, rebleeding gives layering "haematocrit level"). Isodense bilateral SDH is a notorious miss: the sulci are effaced bilaterally and the ventricles look "too small" for age.

Subarachnoid & intraventricular haemorrhage

Traumatic SAH sits in convexity sulci adjacent to a contusion or under a fracture, whereas aneurysmal SAH is centred in the basal cisterns and sylvian fissures. Interpeduncular cistern blood on a trauma scan should still prompt a CTA. IVH in trauma is a marker of severe injury and predicts hydrocephalus; small dependent layering in the occipital horns can be subtle — look at the window (use a narrow "subdural" window of W 150–200 / L 50–70).

Intra-axial injury

Contusions occur where brain hits bone: inferior frontal lobes, anterior temporal poles, and contre-coup opposite the impact site. They "blossom" over 24–72 hours, so a follow-up CT at 6–24 h is standard when the first scan shows contusion. Diffuse axonal injury (DAI) is a shear injury at grey–white junction, corpus callosum (splenium), and dorsolateral brainstem; CT is often normal or shows only petechial foci. MRI with SWI/GRE (haemorrhagic foci) and DWI (non-haemorrhagic foci) is far more sensitive. Grade I = lobar grey–white junction, II = corpus callosum, III = brainstem — grade predicts outcome.

Key points
  • Hyperdense acute blood ~50–70 HU; isodense at 1–3 weeks — the isodense SDH is the classic miss
  • EDH: lentiform, arterial, fracture, doesn't cross sutures, can cross midline. SDH: crescentic, crosses sutures, not falx/tentorium
  • Swirl sign = active bleeding → urgent neurosurgical call
  • Contusions blossom — repeat CT; DAI needs MRI (SWI + DWI)
  • Always report midline shift in mm at the septum pellucidum, basal cistern status, and ventricular size
Never miss / red flags
  • Swirl sign in an EDH or SDH
  • Posterior fossa haematoma of any size
  • Bilateral isodense SDH with "small" ventricles in an elderly patient
  • Effaced basal cisterns / uncal herniation — call before you finish the report
  • Delayed EDH after decompression of a contralateral lesion
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Beam-hardening artefact along the inner table mimicking a thin SDH — check coronal/sagittal
  • Calcified falx or dural calcification mimicking SAH
  • Pseudo-SAH: diffuse cerebral oedema makes the cisterns look relatively dense
  • Prominent CSF spaces in atrophy vs chronic SDH — chronic SDH displaces cortical veins inward; atrophy does not
  • Anaemia makes acute blood isodense
Reporting checklist
  • Type, location, size (max thickness in mm), and mass effect of each collection
  • Midline shift (mm), basal cisterns, ventricles (trapped temporal horn?), herniation
  • Parenchymal contusion/DAI/IVH
  • Skull fracture (bone window), depressed? involving a sinus/carotid canal/temporal bone?
  • Scalp haematoma & foreign bodies; sinus/mastoid fluid (occult base-of-skull fracture)
  • Cervical spine: was it imaged? Recommend if indicated

Brain herniation, stroke & cerebrovascular emergencies

📖 Requisites pp. 10–24FY + Board

Acute ischaemic stroke

The stroke pathway is a time problem. Non-contrast CT first: excludes haemorrhage and looks for early ischaemic signs — loss of grey–white differentiation (insular ribbon sign, obscured lentiform nucleus), sulcal effacement, and the hyperdense vessel sign (dense MCA, MCA "dot" sign in the sylvian fissure). ASPECTS scores ten MCA-territory regions (caudate, lentiform, internal capsule, insula, M1–M6) and subtracts one per involved region; ≤7 predicts poorer outcome after thrombolysis. CTA from arch to vertex identifies large vessel occlusion (LVO), assesses collaterals, and shows tandem carotid disease; CT perfusion separates core (CBV/CBF markedly reduced) from penumbra (prolonged MTT/Tmax with preserved CBV) and extends the thrombectomy window to 24 h in selected patients.

On MRI, DWI is positive within minutes; the DWI–FLAIR mismatch (DWI bright, FLAIR not yet bright) suggests onset <4.5 h in wake-up strokes. Watershed infarcts (rosary pattern in the centrum semiovale, or cortical border zones) point to hypoperfusion or carotid stenosis. Lacunes (<15 mm) are deep small-vessel infarcts.

Haemorrhagic stroke & other vascular emergencies

Spontaneous parenchymal haemorrhage: hypertensive bleeds favour the putamen/external capsule, thalamus, pons and cerebellum; lobar bleeds in the elderly suggest amyloid angiopathy (multiple cortical microbleeds on SWI); a young patient or an atypical location warrants CTA for AVM, aneurysm, dural AV fistula, or venous thrombosis. The CTA spot sign (contrast extravasation into the haematoma) predicts expansion. Haemorrhagic transformation of an infarct is graded HI-1/HI-2 (petechial) and PH-1/PH-2 (parenchymal haematoma; PH-2 is >30% of the infarct with mass effect). Cortical laminar necrosis shows as gyriform T1 hyperintensity weeks after ischaemia — not haemorrhage.

Cerebral venous thrombosis: dense clot in a sinus or cortical vein on NCCT ("cord sign", "dense triangle"), filling defect on CT venogram ("empty delta"), and parenchymal changes that do not respect arterial territories — bilateral parasagittal for the superior sagittal sinus, temporal lobe haemorrhage for the transverse/sigmoid sinus, bilateral thalamic oedema for the deep venous system. Haemorrhagic venous infarcts are common and are not a contraindication to anticoagulation.

Key points
  • Report ASPECTS, hyperdense vessel, and the exact occlusion site; the neurointerventionalist wants M1 vs M2, tandem lesions, and collateral grade
  • Perfusion mismatch (core vs penumbra) drives late-window thrombectomy
  • Infarcts that ignore arterial territories or are bilateral thalamic → think venous
  • Basal cistern SAH → CTA; lobar bleed in an elderly patient → amyloid; young/atypical bleed → CTA/MRI for underlying lesion
Never miss / red flags
  • Hyperdense basilar artery — basilar occlusion is fatal without recanalisation
  • Cerebellar infarct or haemorrhage >3 cm: risk of fourth-ventricle compression and hydrocephalus
  • Spot sign in an ICH
  • Uncal herniation on the first scan
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Old infarct vs new: look for encephalomalacia and ex vacuo dilatation (old) vs mass effect and loss of GW differentiation (new)
  • Dense MCA can be simulated by high haematocrit or calcified atheroma — compare the two sides and use thin slices
  • Pseudo-normalisation of ADC at ~1–2 weeks
  • Arachnoid granulations mimic filling defects in the transverse sinuses

Non-traumatic intracranial emergencies: aneurysm, dissection, hydrocephalus, infection, tumour, white matter

📖 Requisites pp. 20–36FY + Board

Aneurysms, vascular malformations & dissection

Aneurysmal SAH: 85% of spontaneous SAH; NCCT is ~98% sensitive within 6 hours and falls after that, when lumbar puncture or MRI (FLAIR/SWI) is needed. Distribution hints at the site: anterior interhemispheric blood → ACom; sylvian fissure → MCA bifurcation; prepontine → basilar tip/PICA. Modified Fisher grade (thick SAH ± IVH) predicts vasospasm, which peaks at day 4–14 and is monitored with transcranial Doppler and CTA/CTP. Perimesencephalic non-aneurysmal SAH is a benign pattern centred anterior to the brainstem with a negative CTA.

AVM: a tangle of vessels with early draining vein, feeding-artery enlargement, and often calcification; Spetzler–Martin grade uses size, eloquence and deep venous drainage. Dural AV fistula: cortical venous reflux is the dangerous feature. Cavernoma: "popcorn" mixed signal with a complete haemosiderin rim on T2*/SWI, no mass effect, angiographically occult. Developmental venous anomaly ("caput medusae") is benign unless paired with a cavernoma.

Cervicocerebral arterial dissection: crescentic intramural haematoma (T1 fat-sat hyperintense in subacute stage), tapered "flame" occlusion, string sign, or dissecting pseudoaneurysm. Spontaneous dissection favours the ICA 2–3 cm above the bifurcation and the V3 segment of the vertebral artery; young stroke, neck pain, and Horner syndrome are clinical clues. In trauma, screen the neck vessels with CTA when there is a cervical spine fracture through the transverse foramen or C1–C3, a Le Fort II/III or basilar skull fracture involving the carotid canal, a seat-belt sign with neurological deficit, or an unexplained infarct (Denver criteria). Biffl grading: I intimal irregularity <25%, II dissection/haematoma ≥25%, III pseudoaneurysm, IV occlusion, V transection.

Hydrocephalus

Obstructive (non-communicating) hydrocephalus dilates the ventricles proximal to the block: aqueductal stenosis, colloid cyst at the foramen of Monro, posterior fossa mass. Communicating hydrocephalus dilates all ventricles including the fourth (post-SAH, meningitis, NPH). Acute hydrocephalus shows rounded temporal horns (>2 mm), a ballooned third ventricle, and periventricular low density (transependymal CSF flow); the Evans index (frontal horns / max inner skull width) >0.3 is a rough threshold. Ex-vacuo dilatation from atrophy has proportionate sulcal widening and no transependymal flow. Shunt evaluation: compare ventricular size with prior imaging and inspect the entire shunt series for disconnection.

Infection

Meningitis is a clinical/LP diagnosis; imaging is done to exclude mass effect before LP and to find complications (hydrocephalus, subdural empyema, venous thrombosis, infarcts, ventriculitis with dependent debris in the occipital horns). Cerebral abscess: ring-enhancing lesion with a smooth, thin wall that is thinner on the ventricular side, marked surrounding oedema, and central restricted diffusion (the key discriminator from necrotic tumour, which does not restrict centrally). HSV encephalitis: asymmetric medial temporal and insular T2/FLAIR signal with restriction ± haemorrhage, sparing the putamen. Toxoplasmosis vs lymphoma in HIV: toxo is multiple, with "eccentric target" enhancement and no restriction; lymphoma restricts, is often solitary/periventricular, and is thallium/PET avid.

Tumours presenting acutely

Emergencies are haemorrhage into a tumour (metastases from melanoma, RCC, choriocarcinoma, thyroid; primary GBM), obstructive hydrocephalus, and herniation from oedema. Vasogenic oedema spares cortex and follows white matter fingers; the enhancing nodule may be small relative to the oedema. Pituitary apoplexy: acute enlargement of a haemorrhagic pituitary mass with visual loss — look at the sella on every head CT for headache.

White matter disorders

Acute demyelination (MS relapse, ADEM, tumefactive lesion) shows incomplete "open ring" enhancement toward the cortex. PRES: bilateral parieto-occipital vasogenic oedema (cortical and subcortical) in hypertension, eclampsia, or calcineurin inhibitors; DWI usually negative, reversible. Osmotic demyelination: central pontine "trident" T2 signal sparing the periphery and corticospinal tracts, after rapid sodium correction. Wernicke: symmetric medial thalamic, periaqueductal, mammillary body signal in alcoholics/hyperemesis — treat first, image second.

Key points
  • NCCT within 6 h is near-perfect for SAH; after that, LP or MRI
  • Ring lesion with central restriction = abscess until proven otherwise
  • Rounded temporal horns + transependymal oedema = acute hydrocephalus; proportionate sulci = atrophy
  • Open-ring enhancement → demyelination; parieto-occipital oedema in a hypertensive patient → PRES
Never miss / red flags
  • Colloid cyst with acute hydrocephalus — sudden death risk
  • Pituitary apoplexy
  • Basilar tip aneurysm / prepontine SAH
  • Subdural empyema (often small, over the convexity or parafalcine, disproportionate clinical severity)
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Perimesencephalic SAH pattern still needs a CTA before calling it benign
  • Intraventricular metastasis or lymphoma mimicking colloid cyst
  • Post-ictal or migrainous cortical swelling and DWI change mimic stroke — non-territorial and resolve
  • Atrophy-related "enlarged" ventricles in the elderly

Skull & maxillofacial fractures

📖 Requisites pp. 36–44FY + Board

Skull fractures

Linear fractures are lucent, sharp, non-branching lines that do not have the corticated margins of sutures or the branching, tapering course of vascular grooves. Look on thin bone-window reformats and in three planes; fractures parallel to the axial plane (skull base, vertex) are missed on axial images. Report depressed fractures (depth > the calvarial thickness usually means surgery), open/compound fractures (overlying laceration or air), fractures crossing the carotid canal, venous sinuses, or middle meningeal groove, and basilar skull fractures — their indirect signs are pneumocephalus, mastoid or sphenoid sinus fluid, and clinical Battle sign/raccoon eyes. A "growing" skull fracture (leptomeningeal cyst) is a paediatric complication of a diastatic fracture with dural tear.

Orbital fractures

Orbital blow-out: the floor (into the maxillary sinus) and medial wall (into the ethmoids) are the weak points. Report herniation of orbital fat or the inferior rectus muscle ("teardrop" into the sinus), muscle entrapment (rounded, kinked inferior rectus — clinical entrapment is diagnosed by the ophthalmologist, but a trapped muscle in a child is a "white-eyed" emergency), fracture size (>50% of the floor predicts enophthalmos), orbital emphysema, retrobulbar haematoma, and globe injury (flattened globe, lens dislocation, intraocular haemorrhage or foreign body). The trapdoor fracture in children can look almost normal on CT with the muscle held in the sinus.

Zygomaticomaxillary complex & Le Fort

ZMC ("tripod") fracture involves the zygomatic arch, the lateral orbital wall (zygomaticofrontal suture), the inferior orbital rim/orbital floor, and the anterior and posterior maxillary sinus walls — four points, despite the name. Rotation of the zygoma flattens the malar eminence.

A fracture through the pterygoid plates without a Le Fort pattern is usually part of a ZMC or isolated posterior maxillary injury. Naso-orbito-ethmoid (NOE) fractures involve the medial canthal tendon insertion and lacrimal apparatus; "smash" fractures are comminuted pan-facial injuries. Nasal bone fractures are clinical; the CT question is whether the septum, the anterior nasal spine, or the nasofrontal suture is involved.

Mandible

A ring: expect a second fracture (contralateral condyle/angle with a parasymphyseal fracture). Sites: condyle (most common), angle, body, parasymphysis, symphysis, ramus, coronoid. Report displacement, whether the fracture is "open" into a tooth socket, temporomandibular joint dislocation (condyle anterior to the eminence), and airway compromise from a bilateral parasymphyseal ("flail") fracture.

Key points
  • Pneumocephalus or opacified sphenoid/mastoid = base-of-skull fracture until proven otherwise
  • ZMC = 4 points; Le Fort = pterygoid plates + a specific level; look for asymmetry
  • Orbital floor: report muscle/fat herniation, size, globe, and retrobulbar haematoma
  • Mandible: find the second fracture; check the condyles on coronal
Never miss / red flags
  • Retrobulbar haematoma with proptosis — lateral canthotomy within an hour
  • Fracture through the carotid canal → CTA
  • Depressed fracture over a dural venous sinus
  • Paediatric trapdoor orbital fracture with entrapment (oculocardiac reflex)
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Sutures (sphenosquamosal, occipitomastoid, metopic) and vascular grooves mimic fractures; fractures are sharper, uncorticated and often extend to a sinus or a sutural diastasis
  • Normal orbital floor dehiscence vs fracture: fractures have displaced fragments and sinus haematoma
  • Congenital condylar asymmetry vs condylar fracture

Temporal bone fractures, airway & pharyngeal injury

📖 Requisites pp. 44–46FY + Board

Temporal bone fractures are classified by their relation to the long axis of the petrous bone — longitudinal (70–90%; parallel to the petrous ridge, through the external auditory canal and middle ear, causing conductive hearing loss, ossicular dislocation, and haemotympanum) versus transverse (perpendicular; through the otic capsule and internal auditory canal, causing sensorineural loss, vertigo, and facial nerve palsy in ~50%). The more useful modern descriptor is otic-capsule-sparing versus otic-capsule-violating, since capsule-violating fractures carry the higher risk of SNHL, facial paralysis, CSF leak and meningitis. Report the course of the fracture relative to the carotid canal, jugular foramen, facial nerve canal (labyrinthine segment and geniculate ganglion), ossicles (incudostapedial joint most vulnerable), tegmen tympani, and the vestibular aqueduct.

Indirect signs on the trauma head CT that should trigger a dedicated temporal bone CT: opacified mastoid air cells or middle ear, pneumocephalus adjacent to the petrous apex, air in the TMJ, and pneumolabyrinth (air in the cochlea/vestibule — a sign of otic capsule violation).

Airway and pharyngeal injury

Laryngeal trauma: fractures of the thyroid or cricoid cartilage (subtle in the non-ossified young adult), arytenoid dislocation, endolaryngeal haematoma, and paraglottic air. Subcutaneous emphysema in the neck without a visible lung cause should prompt a search for pharyngeal/oesophageal or laryngeal perforation; CT with oral contrast or fluoroscopic oesophagram confirms. Retropharyngeal air or fluid after intubation, endoscopy, or foreign body ingestion suggests perforation, and the retropharyngeal space communicates with the mediastinum via the "danger space" — mediastinitis is the feared complication.

Key points
  • Longitudinal = conductive loss, ossicles; transverse = SNHL, facial nerve. Better: otic capsule sparing vs violating
  • Pneumolabyrinth = capsule violation
  • Neck emphysema without a lung explanation = perforation until proven otherwise
Never miss / red flags
  • Fracture through the carotid canal (CTA)
  • Otic capsule violation with CSF otorrhoea → meningitis risk
  • Expanding neck haematoma or displaced laryngeal fracture — airway
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Normal fissures and canals (petrosquamosal fissure, cochlear aqueduct, subarcuate canal, singular canal) mimic fractures — they are corticated and bilateral
  • Mastoid opacification from otitis rather than fracture

Head & neck infections and non-traumatic emergencies

📖 Requisites pp. 45–58FY + Board

Deep neck space infections

Peritonsillar abscess is the most common deep neck infection: a rim-enhancing collection lateral to the tonsil, medial to the medial pterygoid, displacing the tonsil medially; imaging is usually reserved for atypical cases or failed drainage. Retropharyngeal infection (children — suppurative retropharyngeal nodes; adults — foreign body, instrumentation, spread from tonsils) shows thickening of the prevertebral soft tissue and a rim-enhancing collection between the pharyngeal constrictors and the prevertebral fascia; a non-enhancing effusion can be reactive (adjacent to tonsillitis) rather than pus. The danger space and prevertebral space communicate with the mediastinum, so a low retropharyngeal collection should be followed to the carina. Prevertebral space infection is discitis/osteomyelitis until proven otherwise — inspect the disc and endplates.

Parapharyngeal and masticator space infections are usually odontogenic (mandibular molars) — look for periapical lucency and cortical breach; Ludwig angina is a bilateral cellulitis of the submandibular and sublingual spaces that elevates the tongue and threatens the airway. Lemierre syndrome: pharyngitis → internal jugular vein thrombophlebitis (filling defect with wall enhancement) → septic pulmonary emboli; usually Fusobacterium in a young adult.

Salivary, thyroid, branchial & nodal disease

Sialadenitis with sialolithiasis: enlarged enhancing gland, ductal dilatation, and a hyperdense calculus (submandibular > parotid); look for abscess. Acute thyroiditis is rare (the thyroid resists infection) and suggests a pyriform sinus fistula in a child; thyroid haemorrhage into a nodule or a rapidly enlarging goitre can compress the airway. Infected second branchial cleft cyst: a thick-walled cystic mass anterior to the sternocleidomastoid, posterior to the submandibular gland, lateral to the carotid space — but in an adult over 40 a "cystic neck mass" is cystic nodal metastasis (HPV oropharyngeal SCC, papillary thyroid) until proven otherwise. Suppurative lymphadenitis shows central low density with a thick enhancing rim.

Airway: croup, epiglottitis, angioedema

Croup: subglottic narrowing on the frontal radiograph ("steeple" sign), loss of the normal shoulders, normal epiglottis on the lateral — a clinical diagnosis. Epiglottitis: thickened epiglottis ("thumb" sign) and aryepiglottic folds on the lateral neck; secure the airway before any further imaging — increasingly an adult disease since Hib vaccination. Angioedema (ACE inhibitors, hereditary) causes diffuse submucosal swelling of the tongue, floor of mouth, supraglottis; CT is done to exclude abscess and to plan airway management.

Sinus & orbital infection

Orbital cellulitis is classified relative to the orbital septum: pre-septal (eyelid only) versus post-septal (intraconal/extraconal fat stranding, proptosis, subperiosteal abscess along the lamina papyracea from ethmoid sinusitis). Complications: superior ophthalmic vein thrombosis, cavernous sinus thrombosis (convex lateral wall, filling defects, dilated SOV), intracranial extension (epidural/subdural empyema, frontal lobe abscess — Pott puffy tumour from frontal sinusitis). Invasive fungal sinusitis in the immunocompromised/diabetic patient: bone destruction, extra-sinus fat stranding, and loss of mucosal enhancement ("black turbinate"). Acute mastoiditis: coalescent mastoiditis erodes the septa; subperiosteal abscess, sigmoid sinus thrombosis and Bezold abscess are the complications. Dental disease: periapical abscess, osteomyelitis, and spread to the masticator or submandibular space.

Key points
  • Retropharyngeal space → danger space → mediastinum: scan to the carina
  • Adult "branchial cleft cyst" = necrotic node until proven otherwise
  • Pre-septal vs post-septal cellulitis is the key orbital distinction; look for subperiosteal abscess and SOV thrombosis
  • Lemierre: IJV thrombus + septic emboli in a young adult with a sore throat
Never miss / red flags
  • Ludwig angina or supraglottic swelling — airway first
  • Cavernous sinus thrombosis
  • Invasive fungal sinusitis (black turbinate, bone erosion)
  • Descending mediastinitis
  • Prevertebral collection with discitis / epidural abscess
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Reactive retropharyngeal effusion vs abscess (rim enhancement, mass effect)
  • Normal asymmetric tonsillar lymphoid tissue in children
  • Physiological retropharyngeal thickening in an expiring, flexed infant on the lateral radiograph

Chest trauma: protocol, pleural space, lung contusion & laceration

📖 Requisites pp. 61–67FY + Board
Protocol / technique
  • Trauma CT chest: arterial-phase (bolus-tracked) MDCT from lung apices through the diaphragm, 1–1.5 mm axial with coronal/sagittal reformats; lung, soft-tissue and bone windows; extend to a whole-body "pan-scan" in major mechanism
  • Delayed (venous) phase when there is mediastinal haematoma, suspected active bleeding, or solid-organ injury on the abdominal portion
  • Supine CXR first for tubes/lines, tension, and massive haemothorax — and know its blind spots

Pleural space

Pneumothorax on the supine film hides anteromedially and at the base: look for the deep sulcus sign, a hyperlucent hemithorax or upper quadrant, a sharp cardiac/mediastinal border, a visible anterior costophrenic sulcus, and the "double diaphragm". CT finds occult pneumothorax in up to 40% of trauma patients with a normal CXR — most small (<1 cm) occult pneumothoraces can be observed, but report them because positive-pressure ventilation converts them to tension. Tension: mediastinal shift, flattened/inverted hemidiaphragm, splayed ribs — a clinical emergency treated before imaging. Haemothorax layers posteriorly on the supine film (diffuse haze over the hemithorax); on CT, blood measures 35–70 HU and a fluid–fluid level or active extravasation suggests ongoing arterial bleeding (intercostal, internal mammary). About 200–300 mL is needed before an erect film shows blunting.

Lung contusion and laceration

Contusion: ill-defined ground-glass or consolidation in a non-segmental distribution, typically adjacent to the ribs or spine, often with a subpleural sparing rim of 1–2 mm; appears within 6 hours, peaks at 24–48 h, and resolves by 3–7 days — if it is worsening after day 3, think aspiration, pneumonia or ARDS. Laceration is a tear of the parenchyma that fills with air (pneumatocele), blood (haematoma), or both (air–fluid level); it looks ovoid/round because of lung recoil, is often surrounded by contusion, and takes weeks to months to resolve. Lacerations are classified by mechanism — compression rupture (central, large), compression shear (paravertebral), rib penetration (peripheral, next to a fracture), and adhesion tear.

Key points
  • Supine pneumothorax lives at the base and anterior-medial chest: deep sulcus sign
  • Report occult pneumothorax — ventilation converts it to tension
  • Contusion: non-segmental, subpleural sparing, resolves in days; laceration: round cavity, resolves in weeks
  • Haemothorax with fluid–fluid level or a contrast blush → active bleeding, angiography
Never miss / red flags
  • Tension physiology on the film — call, do not wait for the CT
  • Active intercostal/internal mammary extravasation
  • Large haemothorax with mediastinal shift
  • Contusion pattern worsening after 72 h
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Skin folds and the medial border of the scapula mimic a pleural line — vessels beyond the line and a lucent line that extends outside the thorax exclude pneumothorax
  • Atelectasis and aspiration in dependent lung mimic contusion — contusion is non-anatomic and next to the impact
  • Bullae vs pneumatocele vs traumatic lung cyst

Aortic & great vessel injury, cardiac and oesophageal injury

📖 Requisites pp. 62–66FY + Board

Blunt traumatic aortic injury (BTAI)

Rapid deceleration shears the aorta at points of fixation: the isthmus just distal to the left subclavian origin (~90% of survivors), then the ascending aorta/root (most die at scene), the diaphragmatic hiatus, and the arch branch origins. CXR signs of mediastinal haematoma — mediastinal widening >8 cm at the aortic knob (supine films exaggerate this), loss of the aortic contour, left apical cap, tracheal and NG-tube deviation to the right, depression of the left main bronchus, widened paratracheal or paraspinal stripes, and left haemothorax — are sensitive for haematoma but not specific for aortic injury. A normal CXR does not exclude BTAI in a high-mechanism patient; CTA does.

Great vessel injury (innominate, subclavian, carotid at the origins) occurs with sternal, first-rib and clavicular fractures — report the branch vessels systematically.

Cardiac and pericardial injury

Haemopericardium on CT (fluid >25 HU around the heart) in a trauma patient means cardiac or proximal aortic injury until proven otherwise; pneumopericardium can be from tracheobronchial or oesophageal injury or barotrauma. Myocardial contusion is a clinical/ECG/troponin diagnosis; CT may show a hypoattenuating myocardial segment. Cardiac herniation through a pericardial tear (rare) displaces the heart into the left pleural space. Coronary dissection and valvular injury are seen on ECG-gated CTA.

Oesophageal injury

Rare in blunt trauma, more common in penetrating trauma and iatrogenic instrumentation. Signs: pneumomediastinum (especially with mediastinal fluid), periaortic or paraoesophageal air tracking, pleural effusion (left > right), mediastinal fat stranding, and extraluminal oral contrast on CT oesophagram or fluoroscopy. Missed perforation → mediastinitis with high mortality; a negative water-soluble study should be followed by dilute barium if suspicion persists.

Key points
  • Isthmus, arch vessels, root, hiatus: the deceleration sites
  • Periaortic haematoma with a preserved fat plane between haematoma and aorta is not BTAI
  • SVS grade drives management: I observe, III/IV repair
  • Haemopericardium in trauma = cardiac/aortic injury until excluded
Never miss / red flags
  • Any contour abnormality of the aorta on the trauma CTA — call vascular
  • Pseudoaneurysm with periaortic haematoma
  • Haemopericardium / tamponade physiology
  • Pneumomediastinum with mediastinal fluid (oesophageal perforation)
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Ductus diverticulum: smooth, obtuse-angled bulge at the anteromedial isthmus without haematoma — normal variant
  • Aortic spindle and infundibula of the bronchial/intercostal arteries
  • Pulsation artefact of the ascending aorta mimicking a flap — ECG-gating or checking on the delayed phase solves it
  • Residual thymus or mediastinal fat in the young mimicking haematoma

Tracheobronchial injury, diaphragm rupture & thoracic skeleton

📖 Requisites pp. 67–80FY + Board

Tracheobronchial injury

About 80% of injuries lie within 2.5 cm of the carina, more often the right main bronchus. Signs: persistent pneumothorax and large air leak despite a well-placed chest tube, pneumomediastinum and deep cervical emphysema, abnormal or overdistended endotracheal tube cuff (ETT cuff >2.8 cm), the "fallen lung" sign (collapsed lung falling away from the hilum, dependent posterolaterally, instead of collapsing toward it), and a visible defect or discontinuity of the airway wall on thin-section CT with MPRs/minimum-intensity projections. Bronchoscopy confirms.

Diaphragmatic rupture

Blunt rupture is left-sided in about 75% (the liver protects the right), typically a radial posterolateral tear of the central tendon. CT signs: the "collar sign" (waist-like constriction of a herniated viscus at the defect), the "dependent viscera sign" (herniated bowel or liver lying against the posterior ribs with no interposed lung), direct visualisation of the discontinuous diaphragm, the "hump" and "band" signs on the right (focal liver bulge with a linear hypoattenuating constriction), the "dangling diaphragm" sign (free-floating diaphragm edge curling inward), and segmental non-visualisation of the diaphragm. On the CXR: elevated hemidiaphragm, NG tube curling into the chest, bowel loops above the diaphragm, "mushroom" of herniated stomach. The diagnosis is easily missed on the initial scan, especially on the right and in ventilated patients (positive pressure keeps viscera below), and presents late with strangulation — sagittal and coronal reformats are mandatory.

Thoracic skeleton

Rib fractures: the first three ribs (high energy, check the subclavian vessels and brachial plexus), ribs 9–12 (liver, spleen, kidney injury), and a flail segment (≥3 consecutive ribs fractured in ≥2 places — paradoxical motion). Report the number, side, displacement, and associated pneumothorax/haemothorax/contusion. Sternal fractures are best seen on the sagittal reformat; they are associated with retrosternal haematoma and cardiac contusion but, in isolation, are not a marker of aortic injury. Scapular fractures and sternoclavicular dislocations (posterior dislocation compresses the great vessels, trachea and oesophagus — CT with contrast) indicate high energy. Thoracic spine fractures are frequently overlooked on the chest CT: reformat the spine in bone algorithm and review it as a separate series.

Key points
  • Fallen lung sign, persistent air leak, deep neck emphysema → bronchial rupture
  • Collar, dependent viscera, dangling diaphragm signs — check the diaphragm on coronal and sagittal in every trauma CT
  • Flail segment ≥3 ribs in ≥2 places; first-rib fractures → CTA
  • Posterior SC joint dislocation is a vascular emergency
Never miss / red flags
  • Fallen lung sign
  • Dependent viscera / collar sign — herniated stomach or colon strangulates
  • Posterior sternoclavicular dislocation
  • Thoracic spine fracture with retropulsion on the "chest" CT
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Eventration and diaphragmatic paralysis mimic rupture — no defect, smooth contour
  • Bochdalek hernia (posterior, fat/kidney) vs traumatic hernia
  • Segmental non-visualisation of the diaphragm is common in the elderly without injury
  • Tube-related pneumomediastinum vs airway injury

Blunt abdominal trauma: FAST, CT technique, liver & biliary injury

📖 Requisites pp. 81–89FY + Board

FAST

Four windows — right upper quadrant (Morison pouch), left upper quadrant (splenorenal recess, subphrenic space), pelvis (rectovesical/rectouterine pouch), and subxiphoid (pericardium). FAST detects free fluid, not organ injury; sensitivity for haemoperitoneum is ~80–90% but it misses retroperitoneal, bowel and mesenteric injuries and small volumes, and a negative FAST does not exclude injury in a haemodynamically stable patient who then needs CT. E-FAST adds anterior chest views for pneumothorax (absent lung sliding, "barcode" sign on M-mode). Positive FAST in an unstable patient = laparotomy.

Protocol / technique
  • Portal venous phase (~70 s) CT of the abdomen and pelvis is the standard for blunt trauma; arterial phase is added when vascular injury or active bleeding is suspected (split-bolus or dual-phase protocols)
  • Delayed phase (3–5 min excretory) when there is renal injury, perirenal fluid, or bladder injury (CT cystography with 300–400 mL dilute contrast through the catheter under gravity is the definitive test)
  • Oral contrast is not needed and delays the scan; rectal contrast is used selectively for penetrating flank injury
  • Extravasation that is isodense to the aorta on arterial phase and enlarges/changes on delayed phase = active arterial bleeding; a contained blush that fades and stays in a vessel outline = pseudoaneurysm
  • Lower-density (venous) blush that appears only on delayed phase = venous bleeding

Hepatic injury

The liver is the most commonly injured solid organ in blunt trauma, usually the posterior right lobe (segments 6–8) because of its bulk and the fixation by the coronary ligaments. Injury patterns: subcapsular haematoma (lenticular, flattens the parenchyma), intraparenchymal haematoma, laceration (linear or branching hypodense band — a laceration extending to the porta hepatis or hepatic veins raises the risk of bile leak and vascular injury), and periportal low attenuation (from tracking blood, but also from vigorous fluid resuscitation). AAST grading: I–II are small haematomas/lacerations, III is haematoma >10 cm or laceration >3 cm deep, IV is disruption of 25–75% of a lobe, V is >75% of a lobe or juxtahepatic venous injury (IVC, major hepatic veins), VI is avulsion. Most injuries are managed non-operatively; active extravasation and pseudoaneurysm go to angioembolisation. Delayed complications: biloma (well-defined low-density collection growing over days), haemobilia, hepatic abscess, and delayed haemorrhage.

Gallbladder and bile duct injury

Rare and almost always with liver injury: gallbladder wall thickening or irregularity, pericholecystic fluid, high-density intraluminal blood, a collapsed gallbladder, a mass effect from a haematoma at the gallbladder fossa, or the gallbladder found outside its fossa (avulsion). Bile duct injury is suspected when a laceration involves the porta and free fluid accumulates over days; hepatobiliary scintigraphy (HIDA) or MRCP with hepatobiliary contrast shows the leak. Ultrasound findings are non-specific (thick wall, sludge, pericholecystic fluid).

Key points
  • FAST finds fluid, not injury; a stable patient with a negative FAST still gets CT
  • Portal venous phase is the default; add arterial phase for vascular questions, delayed phase for the kidneys, ureters and bladder
  • Active extravasation vs pseudoaneurysm: the blush that changes on the delayed phase is bleeding
  • Grade V hepatic injury = hepatic vein/IVC involvement — the injury that kills in the operating theatre
Never miss / red flags
  • Active arterial extravasation from the liver — call IR
  • Laceration extending to the hepatic veins or IVC (grade V)
  • Periportal low density plus falling haemoglobin: think delayed haemorrhage or bile leak
  • Free fluid without solid organ injury → look hard at bowel and mesentery
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Periportal oedema from fluid resuscitation vs tracking blood
  • Focal fatty sparing/steatosis and beam hardening from ribs mimic laceration
  • Unopacified hepatic veins on the arterial phase mimic lacerations — check the portal venous phase
  • Congenital fissures and the fissure for the ligamentum venosum

Splenic & pancreatic trauma

📖 Requisites pp. 89–95FY + Board

Splenic injury

The spleen is the most frequently injured organ requiring intervention in blunt trauma. Patterns mirror the liver: subcapsular haematoma, laceration, shattered spleen, and hilar vascular injury. The 2018 AAST revision incorporates CT vascular injury — any active bleeding or pseudoaneurysm/AV fistula within the spleen is at least grade IV; bleeding beyond the capsule into the peritoneum is grade V. Contrast blush that persists and matches vascular density on the delayed phase is a pseudoaneurysm; a blush that enlarges and becomes ill-defined is active bleeding. Non-operative management fails most often with high-grade injuries, large haemoperitoneum, and vascular lesions, hence follow-up CT at 48–72 h in grade III–V injuries. Delayed splenic rupture classically occurs 3–10 days later.

Ultrasound: the spleen is a difficult organ; fresh haematoma is hyperechoic or isoechoic and easily missed; free fluid in the splenorenal recess is the clue. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound is used for follow-up in children.

Pancreatic injury

Rare (2–5% of blunt trauma) but with high morbidity because it is missed. Mechanism: the pancreas is crushed against the spine — neck/body injury in adults (steering wheel), and the "handlebar" injury in children. CT signs are subtle early: peripancreatic fat stranding, fluid between the pancreas and the splenic vein (the most useful sign), focal enlargement, a linear low-density laceration, thickening of the left anterior pararenal fascia, and associated duodenal or left renal injury. The critical question is main pancreatic duct integrity — a laceration involving >50% of the gland thickness implies ductal injury (AAST grade III and above) and needs surgery or ERCP stenting; MRCP or secretin-MRCP evaluates the duct. Repeat CT at 24–48 h if the initial scan is negative but lipase rises. Complications: pancreatitis, pseudocyst, fistula, abscess, and splenic vein thrombosis.

Key points
  • 2018 AAST: any splenic vascular injury (blush, pseudoaneurysm, AVF) is grade IV; extravasation into the peritoneum is grade V
  • Splenic pseudoaneurysm persists with vascular density on delayed imaging; extravasation enlarges and blurs
  • Fluid between the pancreas and splenic vein is the earliest pancreatic injury sign
  • Pancreatic laceration >50% thickness = probable duct injury
Never miss / red flags
  • Active splenic bleeding or pseudoaneurysm — IR
  • Delayed splenic rupture presenting days later with a new haemoperitoneum
  • Pancreatic transection at the neck with duct disruption
  • Duodenal haematoma/perforation accompanying pancreatic injury
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Splenic clefts and lobulations (smooth, corticated, no perisplenic fluid) vs laceration
  • Heterogeneous "zebra" enhancement of the spleen on the arterial phase mimicking injury — check the portal venous phase
  • Unopacified bowel adjacent to the pancreatic tail mimicking a collection
  • Mild peripancreatic stranding in the absence of injury after resuscitation

Renal, ureteral, adrenal, bladder, urethral & rectal trauma; penetrating injury

📖 Requisites pp. 102–114FY + Board

Renal injury

Haematuria plus mechanism drives imaging; gross haematuria, or microscopic haematuria with shock, prompts CT. Portal venous phase plus a 3–5 minute delayed phase (to show urine extravasation). AAST grades: I contusion/subcapsular haematoma; II laceration <1 cm cortical, perinephric haematoma confined to Gerota fascia; III laceration >1 cm without collecting system involvement; IV laceration into the collecting system (urinoma on delayed phase), segmental vascular injury, or renal pelvis/UPJ injury; V shattered kidney or main renal artery/vein avulsion or occlusion (the "cortical rim sign" of a devascularised kidney appears after ~8 hours from capsular collaterals). Pre-existing lesions (hydronephrosis, cysts, tumours) rupture with minor trauma — an occult tumour presenting as a bleed. Medial perinephric haematoma near the hilum → look for a UPJ avulsion (delayed phase shows contrast around the renal pelvis with an unopacified ureter distally).

Ureteral injury

Usually penetrating or iatrogenic; blunt injury is a UPJ avulsion in children and young adults (hyperextension). Delayed-phase CT shows extravasation at the UPJ, an unopacified distal ureter, and a medial urinoma. Missed injury presents late with a urinoma, abscess, or fistula.

Adrenal injury

Right-sided in 75% (compressed between the liver and spine, and the short right adrenal vein transmits IVC pressure). Round or oval hyperdense adrenal haematoma with periadrenal stranding; it resolves over months and can calcify. Bilateral adrenal haemorrhage carries a risk of adrenal insufficiency. An adrenal haematoma in the setting of trauma is almost always accompanied by other ipsilateral injuries (liver, ribs, kidney).

Bladder injury

Pelvic fractures and a full bladder. CT cystography (retrograde filling of 300–400 mL under gravity, with post-drainage images) is the study; passive filling from excreted contrast is not adequate. Extraperitoneal rupture (80–90%): "flame-shaped" contrast in the prevesical space of Retzius, tracking into the thigh, scrotum, or anterior abdominal wall; managed with catheter drainage. Intraperitoneal rupture: contrast outlining bowel loops and in the paracolic gutters, from a dome burst injury — needs surgical repair. Combined injuries occur in ~10%. A bladder wall haematoma or a fracture fragment in the bladder is also reported.

Urethral injury

Male, pelvic fracture (especially straddle injuries and pubic diastasis), blood at the meatus — retrograde urethrogram before catheterisation. Goldman classification: type I stretch injury without extravasation; type II posterior urethra torn above an intact urogenital diaphragm (extravasation into the extraperitoneal pelvis only); type III (most common) injury through the diaphragm, contrast in the perineum and pelvis; type IV bladder neck injury extending into the urethra; type V anterior urethral injury (straddle) with contrast confined by Buck fascia or spreading in the perineum when it is torn.

Rectal injury & penetrating trauma

Rectal injury accompanies pelvic fractures with bone spicules and penetrating perineal trauma: perirectal air or fat stranding, wall thickening, extraluminal rectal contrast. Extraperitoneal rectal air can be from the injury or from tracking pneumomediastinum. In penetrating trauma, "triple-contrast" or IV-only CT maps the wound tract: a tract that reaches the peritoneum, bowel wall thickening or air along the tract, and free fluid define the need for laparotomy in stable patients; retroperitoneal tracts (flank, back) are where rectal contrast helps. Gunshot tracts are traced from entry to exit or retained fragment with a description of every structure crossed.

Key points
  • Delayed phase is mandatory for the kidney and ureter; CT cystography for the bladder
  • Extraperitoneal bladder rupture → catheter; intraperitoneal → surgery
  • Cortical rim sign = devascularised kidney (grade V) presenting after ~8 h
  • Blood at the meatus → RUG before the catheter
Never miss / red flags
  • Renal artery occlusion (warm ischaemia clock is running)
  • Intraperitoneal bladder rupture
  • UPJ avulsion (medial urinoma, non-opacified distal ureter)
  • Bilateral adrenal haemorrhage → adrenal crisis
  • Wound tract crossing the peritoneum
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Renal cortical scarring, duplication, and column of Bertin vs laceration
  • Bladder haematoma vs tumour — clinical history
  • Prevesical "molar tooth" of contrast (extraperitoneal) vs paracolic contrast (intraperitoneal)
  • Pneumomediastinum tracking into the retroperitoneum mimicking rectal or duodenal perforation

Scapula, clavicle, AC joint

Scapular fractures mean high energy — 80–90% have associated injuries (ribs, lung, brachial plexus, subclavian vessels). Elbow, forearm & distal radius

📖 Requisites pp. 122–132FY + Board

Elbow: the fat pads and the lines

On a true lateral flexed to 90°: the anterior fat pad is normally a thin sliver against the coronoid fossa; a raised "sail" anterior fat pad or any visible posterior fat pad means joint effusion — in trauma, an occult fracture (radial head in adults, supracondylar in children) until proven otherwise. The anterior humeral line should pass through the middle third of the capitellum (it passes through the anterior third or misses it in a posteriorly displaced supracondylar fracture), and the radiocapitellar line — drawn along the radial shaft — should pass through the capitellum on every view (it fails in radial head dislocation, including Monteggia). In children, know the ossification order CRITOE (capitellum 1 y, radial head 3, internal/medial epicondyle 5, trochlea 7, olecranon 9, external/lateral epicondyle 11): the trochlea never ossifies before the medial epicondyle, so a "trochlear" ossicle without a medial epicondyle is an avulsed and entrapped medial epicondyle.

Elbow fractures and dislocations

Adults: radial head/neck fractures (most common; Mason I undisplaced, II displaced >2 mm, III comminuted, IV with dislocation), olecranon (transverse, from the triceps pull), distal humerus (intercondylar T/Y, capitellar shear — the "double-arc" sign), and the coronoid process (with the "terrible triad" of posterior dislocation + radial head fracture + coronoid fracture). Dislocation is posterior or posterolateral in 90%; report associated fractures and the brachial artery/median nerve. Essex-Lopresti injury: comminuted radial head fracture with disruption of the interosseous membrane and distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ) — image the wrist.

Forearm

Both-bone fractures are straightforward; the single-bone fracture with a dislocated joint is the trap. Monteggia: proximal ulnar fracture with dislocation of the radial head (Bado type I anterior is most common) — check the radiocapitellar line. Galeazzi: distal-third radial fracture with DRUJ disruption (widened DRUJ, dorsal ulnar displacement on the lateral, ulnar styloid fracture). "Nightstick" fracture: isolated ulnar shaft fracture from a direct blow. The pronator quadratus fat pad on the lateral wrist view bows away from the distal radius or is obliterated with an occult distal radial fracture.

Distal radius

Colles: dorsal angulation and displacement, dorsal comminution, radial shortening, often an ulnar styloid fracture ("dinner fork"). Smith (reverse Colles): volar angulation. Barton: intra-articular shear fracture of the dorsal (or volar — "reverse Barton") rim with carpal subluxation. Hutchinson (chauffeur): radial styloid. Die-punch: depressed lunate fossa. The measurements that matter — radial inclination (normal ~22°), radial height (~11 mm), volar tilt (~11°), ulnar variance, intra-articular step >2 mm — determine the need for reduction and fixation. Always look at the scaphoid and the DRUJ on the same films.

Key points
  • Posterior fat pad = effusion = fracture in trauma
  • Anterior humeral line and radiocapitellar line on every elbow film
  • CRITOE; the trochlea before the medial epicondyle is impossible
  • One forearm bone broken → check both joints (Monteggia, Galeazzi)
  • Distal radius: inclination, height, tilt, articular step; scaphoid on the same films
Never miss / red flags
  • Radial head dislocation missed with an ulnar fracture
  • Terrible triad / elbow dislocation with vascular compromise
  • Volar Barton with carpal subluxation
  • Entrapped medial epicondyle in a child
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Supracondylar process (normal variant) vs fracture
  • Unfused olecranon apophysis vs fracture (smooth, sclerotic margins)
  • Radial head "fracture line" that is actually the normal capitellar edge overlapped on an oblique view
  • Ulnar styloid non-union vs acute fracture

Carpus and hand

📖 Requisites pp. 132–140FY + Board

Scaphoid and other carpal fractures

The scaphoid is the most commonly fractured carpal bone; the waist is the most frequent site and the proximal pole has the poorest blood supply (retrograde flow from the distal pole via the dorsal branch of the radial artery), so proximal-pole fractures have the highest rate of non-union and avascular necrosis. Radiographs are negative in ~15–20% of acute fractures — with snuffbox tenderness, immobilise and repeat at 10–14 days, or go straight to MRI (T1 low-signal line with marrow oedema) or CT. The scaphoid fat stripe is displaced or obliterated with a fracture. Triquetral fracture: a dorsal flake on the lateral view (the second most common carpal fracture). Hamate hook fracture: golf/bat/racquet injury, seen on the carpal tunnel view or CT, causing ulnar nerve symptoms. Lunate fractures are rare; Kienböck disease (AVN of the lunate, often with ulnar negative variance) is the more common lunate problem.

Carpal dislocations: the progressive perilunar instability

Mayfield stages describe the sequence of ligamentous failure around the lunate: I scapholunate dissociation (scapholunate gap >3 mm — "Terry Thomas" sign, scaphoid ring sign from rotary subluxation); II perilunate dislocation (capitate dislocates dorsally, lunate stays in the radial fossa); III lunotriquetral disruption (± triquetral fracture — "trans-triquetral"); IV lunate dislocation (lunate rotates volarly into the carpal tunnel — "spilled teacup" on the lateral, triangular "piece of pie" on the PA). Trans-scaphoid perilunate fracture-dislocation is the most common variant. Gilula's three carpal arcs on the PA view must be smooth; a broken arc and an overlapping lunate/capitate mean dislocation. On the lateral, the radius, lunate, capitate and third metacarpal should be colinear — the capitate seated in the lunate cup.

Carpal instability

Scapholunate angle 30–60° is normal; DISI (dorsal intercalated segment instability) = lunate tilted dorsally, SL angle >60°, capitolunate angle >30°, from scapholunate ligament tear (and progresses to SLAC wrist arthritis); VISI = lunate tilted volarly, SL angle <30°, from lunotriquetral tear. Perform a clenched-fist view to provoke a scapholunate gap.

Hand

Thumb: Bennett fracture (intra-articular fracture of the base of the first metacarpal with the small volar-ulnar fragment held by the anterior oblique ligament while the shaft subluxes proximally and radially from the pull of abductor pollicis longus), Rolando (comminuted Y/T intra-articular base fracture), and the gamekeeper/skier thumb (ulnar collateral ligament avulsion at the proximal phalanx base; a Stener lesion — the torn ligament displaced superficial to the adductor aponeurosis — requires surgery and is an ultrasound/MRI diagnosis). Carpometacarpal dislocations of the fourth/fifth rays are missed on the PA (overlap) and need a 30° pronated oblique or CT — look for loss of the parallel M-shaped CMC joint spaces. Boxer fracture: neck of the fifth metacarpal with volar angulation; report degrees of angulation and rotation. Phalangeal fractures: volar plate avulsion at the middle phalanx base (hyperextension), mallet finger (dorsal base of the distal phalanx, extensor tendon avulsion), jersey finger (FDP avulsion from the volar distal phalanx base — ring finger). Interphalangeal dislocations are usually dorsal; report the post-reduction congruity and any volar plate fragment.

Key points
  • Scaphoid: waist most common, proximal pole worst prognosis; negative films do not exclude it
  • Mayfield I–IV; spilled teacup = lunate dislocation, capitate dorsal = perilunate
  • Gilula arcs and the radius–lunate–capitate line
  • SL angle >60° DISI, <30° VISI
  • Bennett = subluxed shaft with retained volar fragment; Stener lesion = surgery
Never miss / red flags
  • Perilunate/lunate dislocation on a "normal" PA film — check the lateral
  • Scaphoid fracture with proximal-pole displacement
  • CMC dislocation of ring/little fingers
  • Open fracture (air in the soft tissues) of the hand — fight bite
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Bipartite scaphoid (rare) and os centrale vs fracture
  • Normal lunotriquetral overlap on the PA in ulnar deviation
  • Scapholunate gap widened by ulnar deviation or a clenched fist without injury — compare the other side
  • Sesamoids of the thumb

Pelvic ring & acetabular fractures

📖 Requisites pp. 140–148FY + Board

Pelvic ring

The pelvis is a ring: one break usually means another, and the posterior ring (sacrum, SI joints, iliac wings) determines stability and bleeding risk. Isolated injuries that do not disrupt the ring — avulsions (ASIS/sartorius, AIIS/rectus femoris, ischial tuberosity/hamstrings in adolescents), iliac wing (Duverney), single pubic ramus, and transverse sacral fractures below S2 — are stable. Young–Burgess classifies ring disruption by force vector: lateral compression (LC I–III: horizontal pubic rami fractures with sacral impaction, iliac wing fracture, or "windswept" pelvis — most common, lower vascular risk), anteroposterior compression (APC I–III: "open book" — symphyseal diastasis >2.5 cm implies anterior SI ligament disruption; APC III is a completely unstable hemipelvis with the highest transfusion requirement), vertical shear (vertical displacement of the hemipelvis through the sacrum or SI joint, with an L5 transverse process fracture), and combined mechanisms. Tile grades stability: A stable, B partially stable (rotation only), C completely unstable (rotational and vertical).

Sacral fractures are classified by Denis zones: I lateral to the foramina (alar, L5 root injury in ~6%), II through the foramina (sacral root injury ~30%), III into the central canal (neurological deficit >50%, bowel/bladder). Sacral fractures are notoriously invisible on the AP pelvis — the interrupted arcuate lines are the clue — and CT is required. Associated injuries: bladder and urethral injury (report extraperitoneal fluid, catheter position, and recommend cystography/RUG), rectal/vaginal laceration (open pelvic fracture), lumbosacral plexus injury, and arterial bleeding (superior gluteal, internal pudendal, obturator, lateral sacral) seen as arterial-phase extravasation → pelvic angiography/embolisation, or venous plexus/cancellous bleeding managed with a binder and packing. The volume of the pelvic haematoma and its density relative to the vessels point to the source.

Acetabulum

Judet–Letournel describes five elementary fractures (posterior wall — the most common, usually with a posterior hip dislocation; posterior column; anterior wall; anterior column; transverse) and five associated fractures (T-shaped, transverse + posterior wall, posterior column + posterior wall, anterior column + posterior hemitransverse, and both-column — the only pattern where no articular fragment remains attached to the axial skeleton, giving the "spur sign" on the obturator oblique). On the AP radiograph, follow the iliopectineal line (anterior column), the ilioischial line (posterior column), the anterior and posterior rims, the roof, and the teardrop; CT with 3D reformats is the definitive study. Report the fragment size (posterior wall >40% is unstable), roof-arc angles, articular step, intra-articular fragments, and marginal impaction.

Key points
  • Posterior ring = stability and haemorrhage; open-book diastasis >2.5 cm = anterior SI ligament torn
  • LC is the most common mechanism; APC bleeds most
  • Denis zones II–III = sacral roots; the AP film misses sacral fractures
  • Posterior wall is the most common acetabular fracture, with posterior hip dislocation; spur sign = both-column
Never miss / red flags
  • Arterial extravasation in the pelvis
  • Vertically unstable hemipelvis (Tile C / vertical shear)
  • Open pelvic fracture (perineal wound, rectal or vaginal laceration)
  • Bladder/urethral injury with pubic diastasis
  • Posterior hip dislocation with a posterior wall fragment — reduce urgently
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Symphyseal width up to 5 mm normal in adults, more in pregnancy/postpartum
  • Sacral foramina overlapped by bowel gas
  • Insufficiency fractures of the sacrum ("H" sign) and pubic rami in osteoporotic patients — after minor trauma, look for the bilateral pattern
  • Os acetabuli and normal ossification centres in adolescents

Hip, femur & knee

📖 Requisites pp. 148–158FY + Board

Hip dislocation and proximal femur

Hip dislocations are posterior in 85–90% (dashboard injury: hip flexed and adducted; the femoral head lies superolateral and looks smaller, the limb internally rotated) — associated posterior wall acetabular fracture, femoral head fracture (Pipkin classification), sciatic nerve injury, and AVN risk that rises with reduction delay beyond 6 hours. Anterior dislocation (abduction/external rotation) shows the head inferomedial (obturator) or superior (pubic). Post-reduction CT is mandatory for intra-articular fragments and a non-concentric reduction. Proximal femoral fractures: intracapsular (subcapital, transcervical — Garden I incomplete/valgus impacted, II complete undisplaced, III partially displaced, IV completely displaced; III–IV have a high risk of AVN and non-union and get arthroplasty in the elderly) versus extracapsular (intertrochanteric — stable vs unstable by the posteromedial cortex and reverse obliquity; subtrochanteric — within 5 cm below the lesser trochanter, think pathological or atypical bisphosphonate fracture with lateral cortical thickening). Occult fractures in the elderly with negative radiographs: MRI (or CT if MRI unavailable — but CT can miss a non-displaced fracture in osteopenic bone). Shenton line, the trabecular pattern, and a subtle cortical step at the femoral neck are the radiographic clues. Femoral shaft fractures are high energy: look for the ipsilateral femoral neck fracture (missed in ~30% initially), knee injury, and fat embolism.

Knee

A joint effusion on the lateral view is a suprapatellar soft-tissue density; a lipohaemarthrosis (fat–fluid level on a horizontal-beam lateral, or a three-layer fat/serum/blood level on CT/MRI) means an intra-articular fracture with marrow fat leaking into the joint — hunt for the tibial plateau or a patellar/femoral condyle fracture. Tibial plateau fractures (Schatzker I–VI: I lateral split, II lateral split-depression, III pure lateral depression, IV medial plateau — highest energy, with knee dislocation/popliteal injury, V bicondylar, VI with metaphyseal-diaphyseal dissociation) need CT for depression depth (>3 mm) and articular widening (>5 mm). Patellar fractures: transverse (quadriceps pull) with a patella alta, vertical, and comminuted; a bipartite patella (superolateral, smooth margins) is a normal variant. Patellar dislocation is almost always lateral and often self-reduces; the footprint is a medial patellar facet osteochondral fragment and marrow oedema in the lateral femoral condyle with a torn medial patellofemoral ligament — a loose body in the joint is the surgical question. Knee dislocation (multi-ligament injury) has a 20–40% incidence of popliteal artery injury — CTA for any knee dislocation, even one that was reduced in the field, and for a Schatzker IV/VI plateau. Avulsions: Segond (lateral tibial rim, ACL tear), reverse Segond (medial, PCL/MCL), arcuate sign (fibular styloid, posterolateral corner), tibial spine avulsion (ACL in adolescents), tibial tubercle (Osgood territory in adolescents), and the "deep lateral femoral notch" sign (impaction with ACL tear). Distal femoral fractures are supracondylar or intercondylar and, in the elderly, periprosthetic.

Key points
  • Posterior hip dislocation → posterior wall fracture, sciatic nerve, AVN clock (reduce <6 h)
  • Garden III–IV → AVN; subtrochanteric → pathological/atypical
  • Femoral shaft fracture → check the femoral neck
  • Lipohaemarthrosis = intra-articular fracture; Schatzker IV/VI and knee dislocation → CTA
  • Segond = ACL
Never miss / red flags
  • Knee dislocation (spontaneously reduced) with popliteal artery injury
  • Ipsilateral femoral neck fracture with a shaft fracture
  • Occult hip fracture in an elderly patient who cannot bear weight — MRI
  • Open fracture, compartment syndrome
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Bipartite patella vs fracture
  • Fabella, os peroneum-type ossicles, and the cyamella
  • Physiological lucency of the femoral neck (Ward triangle) and the "calcar" overlap
  • Fluid–fluid level from a haemarthrosis without fat is not a fracture proof

Ankle & foot, CTA and 3D reconstruction in extremity trauma

📖 Requisites pp. 158–164FY + Board

Ankle

The ankle ring: medial malleolus/deltoid ligament, lateral malleolus, syndesmosis, posterior malleolus. Weber classifies the fibular fracture relative to the syndesmosis: A below (supination-adduction, syndesmosis intact), B at the level (supination-external rotation, the most common; syndesmosis variably injured), C above (pronation-external rotation, syndesmosis torn — including the Maisonneuve fracture of the proximal fibula with a medial injury, so image the whole leg when the medial clear space is widened without a fibular fracture on the ankle films). Lauge-Hansen relates the mechanism to a staged sequence of injuries. Measure the medial clear space (>4 mm = deltoid rupture/instability), tibiofibular clear space (>6 mm = syndesmotic injury), and tibiofibular overlap (<10 mm on AP = syndesmosis). Talar dome osteochondral fractures and the posterior malleolus (need CT for size >25% of the articular surface) are the missed lesions. Pilon (plafond) fractures are axial-loading intra-articular fractures of the distal tibia (Rüedi–Allgöwer), needing CT for the articular map. Avulsions: anterior tibial tubercle (Tillaux — Chaput fragment; the juvenile Tillaux and triplane fractures in adolescents with a partially closed physis), the Wagstaffe fragment of the fibula, and the base of the fifth metatarsal (peroneus brevis).

Hindfoot

Talar neck fractures (Hawkins I–IV — the risk of AVN rises with subtalar/tibiotalar dislocation; the Hawkins sign of subchondral lucency at 6–8 weeks indicates preserved vascularity), lateral process of the talus fractures (snowboarder), and osteochondral dome lesions. Calcaneal fractures: axial loading (fall from height) — check the spine (10% have a thoracolumbar fracture) and the other calcaneus; Böhler angle <20° (normal 25–40°) and a Gissane angle >130° indicate a depressed posterior facet; Sanders classifies by the number of posterior facet fragments on coronal CT. Subtalar dislocation (medial > lateral) and the total talar extrusion are rare. Achilles rupture is an ultrasound/MRI diagnosis, but the lateral radiograph shows loss of the Kager fat pad triangle.

Midfoot & forefoot

Lisfranc injury: the tarsometatarsal joint complex, keyed by the second metatarsal base recessed between the cuneiforms and held by the Lisfranc ligament (medial cuneiform to second metatarsal base). On the AP, the medial border of the second metatarsal must align with the medial border of the middle cuneiform; on the oblique, the medial border of the fourth metatarsal with the medial border of the cuboid. A gap >2 mm between the first and second metatarsal bases, a "fleck" sign (avulsion at the Lisfranc ligament insertion), dorsal displacement on the lateral, and a cuboid compression fracture ("nutcracker") are the signs; weight-bearing views or CT/MRI find the subtle ones. Homolateral versus divergent dislocations. Navicular fractures (dorsal avulsion, tuberosity, body — stress fractures in athletes), cuboid fractures and the Chopart (midtarsal) fracture-dislocation. Fifth metatarsal: tuberosity avulsion (peroneus brevis, transverse, heals) vs Jones fracture (metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction 1.5–3 cm distal, prone to non-union) vs proximal diaphyseal stress fracture; the normal longitudinal apophysis in adolescents runs parallel to the shaft and is not a fracture. Metatarsal stress fractures (2nd/3rd, "march"), phalangeal fractures, and the sesamoid fractures of the hallux (bipartite sesamoid has smooth margins).

CT angiography and 3D reconstruction

Extremity CTA (arterial-phase, sub-mm acquisition, bolus-tracked in the aorta, run-off to the feet with a delayed pass if opacification is poor) is indicated for hard signs of vascular injury (pulsatile bleeding, expanding haematoma, absent pulse, bruit), soft signs, knee dislocation, penetrating injury near a vessel, and fractures with an abnormal ankle-brachial index (<0.9). Findings: occlusion, active extravasation, pseudoaneurysm, AV fistula, intimal flap/dissection, and vasospasm (smooth tapered narrowing in a young patient). Beware streak artefact from bullets and hardware, and mistiming. Volume-rendered and MPR reconstructions help surgical planning for the acetabulum, tibial plateau, calcaneus, and pilon; MIP for vascular maps; thin curved reformats for the hardware.

Key points
  • Weber A/B/C relates to the syndesmosis; medial clear space >4 mm → Maisonneuve, image the proximal fibula
  • Böhler angle <20° = depressed calcaneus; calcaneal fracture → look at the spine
  • Second metatarsal–middle cuneiform alignment; a fleck sign = Lisfranc
  • Jones vs tuberosity avulsion vs apophysis
  • Knee dislocation and hard signs → CTA
Never miss / red flags
  • Lisfranc injury on "normal" non-weight-bearing films
  • Talar neck fracture with dislocation (AVN)
  • Posterior malleolar fragment >25%
  • Maisonneuve fracture
  • Popliteal/tibial artery occlusion
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Accessory ossicles: os trigonum, os peroneum, os naviculare, os vesalianum, os subfibulare (smooth, corticated)
  • Fifth metatarsal apophysis (longitudinal) vs fracture (transverse)
  • Normal tibiofibular overlap varies with rotation
  • Calcaneal pseudocyst (normal trabecular rarefaction)

Non-traumatic shoulder pain

📖 Requisites pp. 165–170FY + Board

Acute atraumatic shoulder pain in the emergency department is usually calcific tendinitis, rotator cuff disease, acromioclavicular arthropathy, or an inflammatory/crystal arthropathy — but septic arthritis must always be considered when there is fever or the joint is red.

Calcium hydroxyapatite deposition disease

Amorphous, cloud-like calcification within a tendon, most commonly the supraspinatus about 1–2 cm from its insertion; the resorptive phase is the painful one, when the calcification becomes fluffy and ill-defined and may rupture into the subacromial bursa (bursal calcification) or, rarely, into bone (cortical erosion with marrow oedema that mimics a tumour or infection on MRI). Ultrasound-guided barbotage is the treatment. Milwaukee shoulder is the destructive hydroxyapatite arthropathy in elderly women with a large effusion, cuff tear and glenohumeral destruction.

Rotator cuff and impingement

Radiographic clues to a chronic full-thickness cuff tear: acromiohumeral distance <7 mm (superior migration of the head), sclerosis and cysts of the greater tuberosity, and a hooked (type III) acromion or a subacromial spur; os acromiale is a variant associated with impingement. Ultrasound is the first-line test for cuff tears (non-visualisation of the tendon, a hypoechoic full-thickness defect with the deltoid herniating in, a cartilage interface sign, bursal fluid); MRI shows tendon signal and retraction, muscle atrophy/fatty infiltration (Goutallier grade), and the labrum. Acute traumatic tears occur with a dislocation in patients over 40. Subacromial-subdeltoid bursitis alone causes acute pain with fluid in the bursa.

AC and glenohumeral joint disease

AC joint: osteoarthritis (inferior osteophytes that impinge on the cuff), distal clavicular osteolysis (weight-lifters; subchondral cysts and tapering of the distal clavicle with a widened joint), and septic arthritis (rare, IV drug users). Glenohumeral joint: osteoarthritis (inferior humeral osteophyte), rheumatoid and crystal arthropathy, adhesive capsulitis (thickened coracohumeral ligament and rotator interval on MRI, small axillary recess, clinically restricted range — radiographs are normal, which is the point), and septic arthritis/osteomyelitis (effusion, erosions, marrow oedema — aspirate).

Key points
  • Fluffy, ill-defined calcification = resorptive (painful) phase of calcific tendinitis; intraosseous migration mimics tumour
  • Acromiohumeral distance <7 mm = chronic cuff tear
  • Adhesive capsulitis: normal radiographs, thickened coracohumeral ligament
  • Fever + effusion → aspirate before calling it anything else
Never miss / red flags
  • Septic glenohumeral or AC joint
  • Pathological fracture through a proximal humeral lesion presenting as "shoulder pain"
  • Referred pain: Pancoast tumour on the shoulder radiograph, cardiac or diaphragmatic causes
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Intraosseous hydroxyapatite in the greater tuberosity mimicking a tumour
  • Vacuum phenomenon in the glenohumeral joint
  • Calcific tendinitis vs a loose body vs an avulsion fragment

The painful hip without trauma

📖 Requisites pp. 170–176FY + Board

Insufficiency fractures

Normal stress on abnormal bone (osteoporosis, steroids, radiotherapy, renal osteodystrophy, bisphosphonate-associated atypical fractures). Sites around the hip: the femoral neck (compression-side inferomedial sclerotic band vs tension-side superolateral lucent line — tension-side fractures displace and are fixed), the sacral alae (bilateral vertical lines with a horizontal component — the "H" or "Honda" sign on bone scan/MRI), the pubic rami and parasymphyseal bone (often mistaken for aggressive lesions when there is exuberant callus), and the supra-acetabular region (linear sclerosis parallel to the roof). Radiographs are often normal early; MRI (T1 low line surrounded by oedema) is the test.

Transient bone marrow oedema & transient osteoporosis

Diffuse femoral head and neck marrow oedema (T1 low, STIR high) without a subchondral line or a double-line sign, with an effusion, in a middle-aged man or a third-trimester woman; the radiograph shows osteopenia after weeks. Self-limiting over months; it can migrate to the other hip or knee. The differential is early AVN (which shows a subchondral band or focal lesion) and a subchondral insufficiency fracture (a low-signal subchondral line convex to the articular surface).

Osteonecrosis of the femoral head

Risk factors: steroids, alcohol, sickle cell, lupus, HIV, Gaucher, radiation, dysbaric exposure, femoral neck fracture and dislocation. Staging (Ficat/Steinberg/ARCO): stage 0–I radiograph normal, MRI positive; II sclerosis/cysts without collapse; III subchondral fracture (crescent sign) and early collapse; IV flattening and secondary osteoarthritis. The MRI double-line sign (inner high signal granulation, outer low signal sclerosis on T2) is pathognomonic; the "band-like" subchondral lesion is the earliest. Bilateral in 50–80%: image both hips. Extent of the head involved (>30%) predicts collapse.

Arthropathies presenting acutely

Septic arthritis (effusion, then rapid joint-space loss, erosions, and periarticular osteopenia; ultrasound shows the effusion and guides aspiration — the diagnosis is on the fluid, not the image), crystal arthropathy (gout: juxta-articular punched-out erosions with overhanging edges and preserved joint space; CPPD: chondrocalcinosis in the labrum and symphysis with a degenerative pattern), inflammatory arthritis (rheumatoid: symmetric concentric joint-space narrowing, axial migration of the head, protrusio acetabuli), rapidly destructive osteoarthritis of the hip (elderly women, dramatic head loss over months mimicking infection or neuropathic joint), and osteoarthritis flares. Paediatric hip pain is a separate chapter — transient synovitis vs septic arthritis, Perthes, SCFE.

Key points
  • Sacral H sign, parasymphyseal callus, and supra-acetabular sclerosis are insufficiency fractures, not metastases
  • Transient marrow oedema: diffuse, no subchondral line; AVN: focal, double line; subchondral insufficiency fracture: convex subchondral line
  • Image both hips for AVN
  • Aspirate a hot hip
Never miss / red flags
  • Tension-side (superolateral) femoral neck insufficiency fracture — it will displace
  • Septic arthritis of the hip (adult or child)
  • Pathological fracture through a metastasis
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Subchondral insufficiency fracture misdiagnosed as AVN
  • Bilateral sacral insufficiency fractures reported as metastases
  • Herniation pits of the femoral neck (normal)
  • Physiological pelvic marrow signal in young adults

Appendicular musculoskeletal infection & foreign bodies

📖 Requisites pp. 176–185FY + Board

Soft-tissue infection

Cellulitis: skin thickening and subcutaneous fat stranding/reticulation on CT, ultrasound "cobblestoning" — non-specific and identical to oedema. The imaging question is whether there is an abscess (rim-enhancing fluid collection; on ultrasound a hypoechoic collection with mobile debris and no internal flow, compressible/swirling with probe pressure) that needs drainage. Necrotising fasciitis: soft-tissue gas along fascial planes (highly specific but present in only ~50%), fascial thickening and fluid tracking along deep fascia, muscle involvement, and non-enhancement of necrotic fascia — a surgical emergency; do not delay debridement for imaging. Pyomyositis: intramuscular abscess (tropical, HIV, diabetics, IV drug users), usually the thigh/gluteal muscles. Infected bursae (olecranon, prepatellar) and tenosynovitis (fluid distending the tendon sheath; flexor tenosynovitis of the hand is a surgical emergency — Kanavel signs). Diabetic foot: cellulitis, ulcer, sinus tract, abscess, osteomyelitis, and the neuropathic joint — MRI with a marker over the ulcer.

Septic arthritis

Haematogenous or direct inoculation; a monoarticular hot joint is septic until the aspirate says otherwise. Radiographs: effusion, periarticular osteopenia, then uniform joint-space loss and marginal/central erosions within 1–2 weeks (much faster than rheumatoid or gout). Ultrasound detects the effusion (child's hip: capsule-to-bone distance >5 mm or >2 mm side difference) and guides aspiration; MRI shows synovitis, marrow oedema on both sides of the joint (reactive vs osteomyelitis is hard), and periarticular abscess. Gonococcal, staphylococcal, and in the sternoclavicular/sacroiliac joints of IV drug users, Pseudomonas.

Acute osteomyelitis

Haematogenous osteomyelitis seeds the metaphysis in children (the transphyseal vessels are open in infants under 18 months, so epiphyseal and joint involvement occur) and the vertebra/diabetic foot in adults; contiguous spread from an ulcer or open fracture in adults. Radiographs lag 10–14 days: deep soft-tissue swelling with loss of fat planes is the earliest sign, then periosteal reaction, permeative lucency, and cortical destruction (30–50% bone loss before lucency is visible). MRI is the test — T1 low marrow signal replacing fat (the most specific sign), STIR high, enhancement, subperiosteal abscess, intraosseous abscess (Brodie abscess with the "penumbra" sign on T1), cortical breach, sinus tract, and sequestrum (dead bone, low signal on all sequences, better on CT). Bone scan and labelled leukocyte scintigraphy are used when MRI is impossible (hardware). Chronic osteomyelitis: sequestrum, involucrum, cloaca. Diabetic foot: the ulcer-adjacent bone with T1 marrow replacement is osteomyelitis; oedema alone (T1 normal) is reactive; the neuropathic (Charcot) joint favours the midfoot with fragmentation, dislocation and debris, and in the absence of an ulcer or sinus, is not infection.

Foreign bodies and infected hardware

Radiographs show metal, glass (nearly all glass is radiopaque), gravel and most stone; wood, plastic and thorns are radiolucent and best found by ultrasound (a linear echogenic focus with posterior shadowing or reverberation, and a surrounding hypoechoic halo of granulation tissue/pus after 24 hours). CT finds most bodies except thin wood; MRI is contraindicated until metal is excluded. Infected orthopaedic hardware: lucency around the implant >2 mm or progressive, periosteal reaction, fluid collections tracking to the hardware, and loosening — differentiate from mechanical loosening; nuclear medicine (labelled WBC + marrow scan) or PET is used when MRI artefact limits assessment. Metal-artefact-reduction sequences help.

Key points
  • Fascial gas = necrotising fasciitis (specific, insensitive); its absence does not exclude it
  • Septic arthritis destroys a joint in days: aspirate
  • Osteomyelitis on MRI = T1 marrow replacement; oedema alone is reactive
  • Wood is radiolucent — ultrasound; glass is radiopaque
Never miss / red flags
  • Necrotising fasciitis
  • Septic arthritis of a large joint
  • Flexor tenosynovitis of the hand
  • Subperiosteal abscess in a child (decompression)
  • Retained foreign body next to a neurovascular bundle
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Gas from an open wound or a recent injection mimicking necrotising infection
  • Reactive marrow oedema next to an ulcer mislabelled as osteomyelitis
  • Charcot neuroarthropathy vs osteomyelitis
  • Post-surgical marrow and soft-tissue changes for months after arthroplasty

Paediatric GU & scrotal/pelvic emergencies

📖 Requisites pp. 197–204FY + Board

Posterior urethral valves: the most common cause of bladder outlet obstruction in boys — bilateral hydroureteronephrosis, a thick-walled trabeculated bladder, and a dilated posterior urethra on the voiding cystourethrogram ("keyhole" sign on ultrasound); oligohydramnios and renal dysplasia when severe. Urinary tract infection: ultrasound for hydronephrosis, abscess, and pyonephrosis; acute pyelonephritis shows focal echogenic or hypoperfused parenchyma (DMSA is the definitive test for scarring). Hydronephrosis is graded (SFU 0–4); UPJ obstruction is the most common congenital cause (dilated pelvis with a non-dilated ureter; MAG3 diuretic renography to prove obstruction), then vesicoureteral reflux (VCUG grades I–V) and ureterocele/duplex systems. Urolithiasis: ultrasound first in children (twinkling artefact, hydronephrosis), low-dose CT if needed.

Scrotum

Testicular torsion: peak in adolescence (bell-clapper deformity) and the neonatal period (extravaginal). Ultrasound: absent or asymmetrically decreased intratesticular flow (colour and spectral Doppler; compare with the other side using the same settings), an enlarged heterogeneous testis (hypoechoic = late/infarcted), the spermatic cord "whirlpool" sign at the external ring (the most specific finding), and a reactive hydrocele. Partial or intermittent torsion can show preserved arterial flow with absent venous flow or a high-resistance waveform. Salvage falls from ~90% at 6 hours to <10% after 24 hours. Torsion of the testicular appendage (appendix testis or epididymis): pre-pubertal boy, a "blue dot", an enlarged avascular nodule with surrounding hyperaemia — conservative. Epididymitis/epididymo-orchitis: enlarged hyperaemic epididymis (± testis), reactive hydrocele, scrotal wall thickening; in pre-pubertal boys think urinary tract anomaly. Scrotal hernia: bowel loops or omentum in the scrotum, peristalsis; incarceration → strangulation.

Female pelvis

Adnexal torsion: enlarged ovary (>4 cm, or twice the volume of the other side) with peripheralised follicles ("string of pearls"), stromal oedema, a twisted vascular pedicle ("whirlpool"), midline position, free fluid; Doppler flow may be present because of the dual arterial supply — absent flow is specific, present flow does not exclude torsion. Underlying lesions in about half (dermoid, cyst). Haemorrhagic ovarian cyst: complex cyst with a reticular "fishnet" or "lace-like" internal pattern, a retracting clot, no internal flow, and a ring of peripheral flow; resolves on follow-up in 6–8 weeks. Haematocolpos/haematometrocolpos: imperforate hymen in an adolescent with primary amenorrhoea and cyclical pain — a distended vagina (± uterus) filled with echogenic blood behind the bladder.

Key points
  • Torsion: whirlpool of the cord is the most specific sign; flow can be present early or with intermittent torsion
  • Appendage torsion: blue dot, small avascular nodule with surrounding hyperaemia — no surgery
  • Ovarian torsion: big ovary, peripheral follicles, flow may persist
  • PUV: keyhole sign on ultrasound, confirmed on VCUG
Never miss / red flags
  • Testicular torsion — do not delay surgery for imaging when the clinical picture is clear
  • Ovarian torsion
  • Pyonephrosis in an obstructed system (nephrostomy)
  • Bilateral hydronephrosis with a thick bladder in a male infant (PUV)
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Low-flow Doppler settings failing to show normal paediatric testicular flow → false torsion
  • Epididymitis with preserved flow does not exclude concurrent torsion
  • Physiological corpus luteum vs haemorrhagic cyst vs torsion

Paediatric CNS & musculoskeletal emergencies, including non-accidental injury

📖 Requisites pp. 210–220FY + Board

CNS

Skull fractures in infants: linear and parietal are typical of short falls; multiple, bilateral, depressed, diastatic, or occipital fractures raise concern for abuse. Hypoxic-ischaemic injury: in the term neonate, the deep grey nuclei and perirolandic cortex (severe) or the parasagittal watershed (mild-moderate); in the preterm, periventricular leukomalacia and germinal matrix haemorrhage (grades I–IV on cranial ultrasound). Hydrocephalus in infants: bulging fontanelle, head circumference crossing centiles, cranial ultrasound then MRI. Venous sinus thrombosis: dehydration, otitis/mastoiditis, coagulopathy. Meningitis/encephalitis and their complications (subdural effusion or empyema, infarcts, hydrocephalus, ventriculitis); herpes encephalitis in neonates is diffuse rather than temporal. Cervical spine injury in children: upper cervical (C1–C3) and ligamentous injuries, SCIWORA (MRI), and the normal variants — pseudosubluxation of C2 on C3 (Swischuk line: the spinolaminar line of C1–C3 should pass within 2 mm of the anterior cortex of the C2 posterior arch), a widened predental space up to 5 mm, wedged C3 body, and unfused synchondroses/os odontoideum. Mastoiditis with subperiosteal abscess or sigmoid sinus thrombosis; cervical adenitis and suppurative nodes.

Musculoskeletal

Salter–Harris physeal fractures (SALTR mnemonic): I through the physis alone (Slipped, e.g. SCFE, often radiographically occult — look for physeal widening); II physis + metaphysis (Above; the most common, ~75%); III physis + epiphysis (Lower; intra-articular, growth arrest risk); IV through metaphysis, physis and epiphysis (Through); V crush of the physis (Rammed; diagnosed retrospectively by growth arrest). Higher grades and the distal femur/proximal tibia carry the highest growth-arrest risk. Plastic bowing (bending without a visible cortical break — compare with the other side), buckle/torus (compression of one cortex at the metaphysis), and greenstick (one cortex broken, the other bent) are the incomplete fractures of childhood. Toddler fracture: a non-displaced spiral tibial fracture in a walking toddder (1–3 years), often invisible initially. Elbow: supracondylar (anterior humeral line, posterior fat pad; Gartland I–III), lateral condyle (Milch), medial epicondyle avulsion (± entrapment), radial neck. Hip pain: transient synovitis (effusion, well child) vs septic arthritis (Kocher criteria — fever, non-weight-bearing, ESR, WCC; aspirate), Perthes (4–8 years, sclerotic flattened epiphysis, subchondral fracture — Waldenström), SCFE (10–15 years, obese, Klein line fails to intersect the epiphysis on the frog-leg lateral, Southwick angle; bilateral in 20–40%; slip is a Salter I), and osteomyelitis.

Non-accidental injury

Skeletal survey (not a babygram) under 2 years: skull, chest AP and both obliques, spine, pelvis, each long bone, hands and feet — repeat at 2 weeks for healing fractures. High-specificity fractures: classic metaphyseal lesions (corner/bucket-handle — shearing of the metaphysis by twisting/pulling), posterior rib fractures (squeezing), scapular, spinous process and sternal fractures. Moderate specificity: multiple fractures of different ages, epiphyseal separations, vertebral body fractures, digital fractures, complex skull fractures. Any fracture in a non-ambulatory infant without a credible mechanism is suspicious. Abusive head trauma: subdural haematomas of different ages, interhemispheric SDH, retinal haemorrhages (ophthalmology), hypoxic-ischaemic injury, and cervical spine ligamentous injury — MRI brain and spine. Abdominal injury: duodenal haematoma, pancreatic injury, liver laceration in a toddler without a history. Differentials to document: osteogenesis imperfecta, rickets, metabolic bone disease of prematurity, Menkes, birth trauma (clavicle, humerus), and normal variants (metaphyseal spurs/beaks, physiological periostitis at 1–6 months).

Key points
  • Salter–Harris SALTR; II most common; V is retrospective
  • Toddler fracture and buckle fractures are subtle — oblique views and follow-up
  • Klein line and the frog-leg lateral for SCFE; image both hips
  • Classic metaphyseal lesions and posterior rib fractures are the highest-specificity NAI fractures; skeletal survey + repeat at 2 weeks
  • Subdurals of different ages = abusive head trauma until proven otherwise
Never miss / red flags
  • Septic arthritis of the hip in a child
  • Unstable SCFE
  • Supracondylar fracture with vascular compromise (brachial artery)
  • Non-accidental injury — document everything, communicate directly
  • C1–C2 injury or SCIWORA
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Pseudosubluxation C2/C3 and the wide predental space in children
  • Physiological periosteal reaction in infants
  • Metaphyseal variants (spurs, step-offs) vs corner fractures
  • Rickets (frayed, cupped metaphyses) mimicking NAI

Cervical spine trauma: algorithms, craniocervical junction & subaxial injuries

📖 Requisites pp. 221–232FY + Board

Imaging algorithms

Clinical decision rules (NEXUS: no midline tenderness, no focal deficit, normal alertness, no intoxication, no distracting injury; Canadian C-spine rule adds mechanism, age ≥65, and the ability to rotate 45° each way) decide who needs imaging. In adults, multidetector CT with sagittal and coronal reformats has replaced radiographs (sensitivity ~98% vs ~50% for the three-view series). MRI is added for neurological deficit, suspected ligamentous injury or cord injury (SCIWORA), and an unreliable examination in the obtunded patient — although a high-quality negative CT in an obtunded adult is increasingly accepted to clear the collar. CTA of the neck follows fractures through the transverse foramen, C1–C3 fractures, subluxation, and facet dislocation (Denver criteria).

Craniocervical junction

Atlanto-occipital dissociation: usually fatal; survivors show a basion–dens interval >12 mm, a basion–axial (posterior axial line) interval >12 mm, the Powers ratio >1, prevertebral swelling, and haematoma around the tectorial membrane on MRI. Occipital condyle fractures (Anderson–Montesano: I impaction, II extension of a skull base fracture, III alar ligament avulsion — the unstable one). Atlas (C1) fractures: Jefferson burst (axial load, lateral masses displaced outward; combined lateral mass overhang >7 mm on the open-mouth view or CT implies transverse ligament rupture and instability), posterior arch fracture (hyperextension, usually stable), and anterior arch fractures. Atlantoaxial subluxation: atlantodental interval >3 mm in adults (>5 mm in children) implies transverse ligament rupture; rotatory fixation (Fielding). Odontoid fractures: type I tip (rare), type II base of the dens (most common, non-union risk in the elderly, unstable), type III extending into the C2 body (better healing); the elderly fall with a hyperextension mechanism and a type II dens fracture is the classic injury. Hangman fracture: traumatic spondylolisthesis of C2 — bilateral pars fractures from hyperextension (Levine–Edwards grades by displacement and angulation; type II with disc disruption and type III with facet dislocation are unstable).

Prevertebral soft tissues

Prevertebral soft-tissue thickness: <7 mm at C2 and <21 mm at C6 (or <half a vertebral body width above C4, <a full body width below) on the lateral radiograph; CT sagittal reformats show focal swelling or haematoma. Widening is a sensitive but non-specific sign of anterior column injury, particularly hyperextension injuries in the elderly whose CT may otherwise look "degenerative".

Thoracolumbar spine trauma, stability, soft-tissue injury & pitfalls

📖 Requisites pp. 232–237FY + Board

Injury patterns

The thoracolumbar junction (T11–L2) is the transition from the rigid thoracic to the mobile lumbar spine and takes most injuries. Compression fracture: anterior column only, anterior wedge with an intact posterior body wall — >50% height loss or >20–30° kyphosis raises concern for posterior ligamentous injury. Burst fracture: axial load fracturing the anterior and middle columns with retropulsion into the canal, widening of the interpedicular distance on the AP view, laminar fractures (with possible dural tear and trapped nerve roots), and canal compromise. Chance (flexion-distraction) fracture: a horizontal fracture through the posterior elements, pedicles, and body (or its purely ligamentous equivalent through the disc and posterior ligaments) from a lap belt — "empty" vertebral body on axial CT, widened interspinous distance, and the association with bowel/mesenteric and pancreatic injury (50%). Fracture-dislocation: all three columns with translation or rotation, the most unstable, with a high rate of neurological injury. The Denis three-column concept (anterior: anterior longitudinal ligament and anterior two-thirds of the body/disc; middle: posterior third and posterior longitudinal ligament; posterior: pedicles, facets, laminae, spinous process and ligaments) underlies the definition of instability as two or more columns disrupted.

Severity and treatment decisions

TLICS scores morphology (compression 1, burst 2, translation/rotation 3, distraction 4), the posterior ligamentous complex (intact 0, indeterminate 2, disrupted 3), and neurology (intact 0, root 2, complete cord 2, incomplete cord/cauda equina 3): a total ≤3 is non-operative, 4 is indeterminate, ≥5 is surgical. The AO Spine classification (A compression, B distraction/tension band, C translation) is the alternative. MRI defines the posterior ligamentous complex (fluid signal in the interspinous space and discontinuity of the supraspinous ligament/ligamentum flavum on STIR), cord signal (oedema vs haemorrhage — haemorrhage predicts a worse outcome), epidural haematoma, disc herniation, and vertebral artery injury; it also identifies the acute fracture among multiple old osteoporotic compressions (marrow oedema).

Pitfalls

Fractures parallel to the axial plane (Chance) are missed on axial images and found on the sagittal; nutrient channels, limbus vertebra (anterosuperior apophyseal separation, corticated), Schmorl nodes, and Scheuermann wedging mimic injury; transverse process fractures are markers of high energy and are associated with abdominal injuries; the "trauma" chest and abdominal CT must be reformatted in the sagittal plane in bone algorithm and the spine read as its own series — up to a third of thoracolumbar fractures are discovered this way. In osteoporotic patients, distinguishing an acute fracture from a chronic one needs MRI (or a previous study). Count vertebrae carefully and localise levels reproducibly.

Spinal soft-tissue injury

Spinal cord injury: cord oedema (length predicts outcome), haemorrhage (hypointense on GRE/T2), transection; central cord syndrome in the elderly hyperextension injury with pre-existing stenosis. SCIWORA in children. Epidural haematoma (biconvex, T1 iso-/hyperintense, displaces the dura); traumatic disc herniation (a large fragment behind a fracture is a reason to decompress anteriorly before reducing a dislocation); ligamentous injury (ALL, PLL, interspinous); dural tear with CSF leak and nerve-root entrapment in a laminar fracture; and vertebral artery dissection.

Key points
  • Compression = anterior column; burst = anterior + middle with retropulsion and widened pedicles; Chance = horizontal through all three with a seat belt; fracture-dislocation = translation
  • TLICS: morphology + PLC + neurology; ≥5 operate
  • Always reformat and read the spine on the trauma chest/abdomen CT
  • Chance fracture → look at the bowel and pancreas
  • MRI decides acute vs old in osteoporosis and shows the PLC
Never miss / red flags
  • Retropulsed fragment with cord/conus compression and deficit
  • Fracture-dislocation
  • Chance fracture with associated abdominal injury
  • Epidural haematoma with progressive deficit
  • Multiple non-contiguous fractures (10–15%) — image the whole spine
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Limbus vertebra and Schmorl nodes
  • Old osteoporotic wedge vs acute fracture
  • Chance fracture invisible on axial images
  • Physiological wedging of T11–L1 (anterior height up to ~10% less)

Non-traumatic spine emergencies

📖 Requisites pp. 237–242FY + Board

The non-traumatic spinal emergency is cord or cauda equina compression, whatever the cause; MRI of the entire spine with gadolinium is the test, and the report must state the level, the compartment (extradural, intradural-extramedullary, intramedullary), the degree of compression, and cord signal change.

Degenerative

Cauda equina syndrome from a large central disc extrusion (L4/5, L5/S1), or a sequestered fragment; acute cord compression from a thoracic disc or cervical disc herniation in a stenotic canal; synovial cysts; spontaneous spinal epidural haematoma (anticoagulated, older patient with sudden back pain and deficit — dorsal extradural collection, T1 iso/hyperintense, T2 heterogeneous, no enhancement or a thin rim). Report the fragment, the canal compromise and root/cord contact.

Neoplasms and tumour-like conditions

Metastatic epidural cord compression (breast, lung, prostate, myeloma, lymphoma): vertebral body replacement (T1 low, no fat), posterior element involvement, epidural soft tissue, and a "curtain"/"draped" appearance from the PLL. Pathological compression fracture vs osteoporotic: convex posterior border, pedicle involvement, epidural mass, diffuse marrow replacement, and restricted diffusion favour malignancy; a retropulsed fragment with a horizontal band of oedema and preserved marrow signal favours benign. Intradural tumours (schwannoma/meningioma, ependymoma, astrocytoma, drop metastases) present acutely less often. Aneurysmal bone cyst, haemangioma (rarely aggressive with epidural extension), and Paget disease with cord compression.

Inflammation and demyelination

Transverse myelitis: a long segment (>2 vertebral bodies) of central cord T2 signal with expansion and patchy enhancement; in neuromyelitis optica (AQP4) it is longitudinally extensive; MS plaques are short, eccentric/dorsolateral, and rarely more than two segments. Acute cord infarction (anterior spinal artery — after aortic surgery/dissection, hypotension, embolism): "owl-eye" anterior horn T2 hyperintensity with restricted diffusion, sparing the posterior columns, often with an adjacent vertebral body infarct.

Infection

Pyogenic spondylodiscitis: destruction of the endplates on either side of a disc with loss of the disc height, T1 low and STIR high marrow on both sides, disc enhancement, paraspinal phlegmon/psoas abscess, and epidural abscess (the emergency — a rim-enhancing collection compressing the thecal sac; MRSA, IV drug use, diabetes, instrumentation). Tuberculous spondylitis: relative sparing of the disc, subligamentous spread over several levels, large calcified paraspinal abscesses, gibbus deformity, and skip lesions. Epidural abscess can also occur without discitis (haematogenous, post-epidural anaesthesia). Facet joint septic arthritis is an under-recognised source.

Vascular

Spinal dural AV fistula (older men, progressive myelopathy, long-segment cord oedema with flow voids on the cord surface — the "missing piece" cause of an unexplained myelopathy; CTA/MRA then catheter angiography); spinal epidural or subdural haematoma; spinal cord infarction; aortic dissection with cord ischaemia; and spinal SAH from an AVM or aneurysm.

Key points
  • Whole-spine MRI with contrast for any suspected cord/cauda compression; report level, compartment, compression, and cord signal
  • Epidural abscess and spontaneous epidural haematoma are the extradural emergencies — anticoagulation and infection risk factors point the way
  • Pathological vs osteoporotic fracture: convex posterior border, pedicles, epidural mass, diffusion
  • Cord infarct: owl eyes, restricted diffusion, adjacent vertebral infarct; NMO is longitudinally extensive
Never miss / red flags
  • Cauda equina syndrome (disc, haematoma, abscess, tumour)
  • Epidural abscess
  • Metastatic cord compression
  • Spontaneous epidural haematoma in an anticoagulated patient
  • Spinal cord infarction after aortic intervention
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Modic type 1 endplate change vs early discitis (discitis involves the disc with enhancement and paraspinal changes)
  • Haemangioma vs metastasis (fat on T1, polka dots on CT)
  • Physiological epidural fat/venous plexus vs haematoma
  • Artefactual central cord "signal" from CSF flow

Non-traumatic thorax: chest radiography and CT of the parenchyma

📖 Requisites pp. 243–251FY + Board

Pneumonia

Patterns: lobar (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Klebsiella — bulging fissure), bronchopneumonia (patchy, multifocal, Staphylococcus), interstitial/atypical (Mycoplasma, viral — perihilar reticular and ground-glass), aspiration (dependent segments: posterior upper and superior lower lobes supine, basal lower lobes erect; right > left), and cavitating (anaerobes, Staphylococcus, TB, fungal). The silhouette sign localises: loss of the right heart border = right middle lobe/lingula; loss of the hemidiaphragm = lower lobe; loss of the aortic knob = left upper lobe. Complications: parapneumonic effusion/empyema (split-pleura sign of enhancing thickened visceral and parietal pleura, lenticular shape, loculation), abscess (thick irregular wall, air–fluid level, acute angle with the chest wall — unlike empyema), and necrotising pneumonia. Immunocompromised: Pneumocystis (bilateral perihilar ground-glass, cysts, pneumothorax), fungal (halo sign of angioinvasive aspergillosis, reverse halo of mucormycosis/organising pneumonia), CMV, and TB (upper-lobe cavitation, tree-in-bud endobronchial spread, miliary nodules).

Heart failure and oedema

The sequence with rising pulmonary venous pressure: upper-lobe venous diversion (cephalisation; PCWP ~12–18 mmHg), interstitial oedema (Kerley B lines, peribronchial cuffing, thickened fissures, indistinct vessels; 18–25 mmHg), then alveolar oedema (perihilar "bat-wing" airspace opacity, effusions; >25 mmHg). Cardiomegaly (CTR >0.5 on PA) and effusions favour cardiogenic; a normal heart size with acute oedema suggests acute mitral regurgitation, MI, arrhythmia, or non-cardiogenic oedema (ARDS — peripheral, no effusions, no cardiomegaly; neurogenic; re-expansion; negative-pressure; drug/inhalational). Asymmetric oedema: right upper lobe in mitral regurgitation, unilateral with contralateral emphysema or PE. CT of oedema: smooth septal thickening, ground-glass with a gravity-dependent gradient, effusions, dilated pulmonary veins.

CT of the pulmonary parenchyma and CT technique

Distinguish the pattern: consolidation (obscured vessels), ground-glass (vessels visible), nodules (centrilobular vs perilymphatic vs random), septal thickening, cysts, and cavities. Acute diffuse ground-glass in the ED: oedema, haemorrhage (drop in Hb), infection (viral, PCP), and acute eosinophilic pneumonia; an "organising" pattern of peripheral/peribronchial consolidation suggests OP; diffuse alveolar damage (ARDS) is dependent dense consolidation with anterior ground-glass. Technique: non-contrast HRCT for diffuse disease; contrast-enhanced venous phase for infection and effusion; CT pulmonary angiography for PE (bolus-tracked in the main pulmonary artery, ~20 s, inspiration held to avoid transient interruption of contrast from the IVC); ECG-gated CTA for the aorta and coronaries; dual-energy iodine maps for perfusion; and low-dose protocols where possible. Report incidental findings that matter to the acute admission (nodules for follow-up per Fleischner, coronary calcification, adrenal masses).

Key points
  • Silhouette sign localises; aspiration goes to the dependent segments
  • Empyema: lenticular, split pleura, obtuse angle; abscess: round, thick wall, acute angle
  • Cephalisation → Kerley B → bat-wing; normal heart + oedema = acute MR/MI/non-cardiogenic
  • Halo = angioinvasive aspergillosis; reverse halo = mucor/OP; tree-in-bud = endobronchial infection/TB
  • CTPA timing: pulmonary artery bolus, ~20 s
Never miss / red flags
  • Empyema needing drainage
  • Bulging fissure / necrotising pneumonia
  • Miliary TB (immunocompromised)
  • Unilateral "pneumonia" that is actually an obstructing tumour (no air bronchograms, volume loss, hilar mass)
  • Transient interruption of contrast producing a non-diagnostic CTPA — repeat rather than report
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Pleural effusion layering posteriorly on the supine film mimicking consolidation
  • Breast shadows and nipple shadows
  • Dependent atelectasis vs early consolidation on CT
  • Lipoid pneumonia (fat attenuation), pulmonary haemorrhage vs oedema

Pleura, pericardium & mediastinum

📖 Requisites pp. 251–255FY + Board

Pleura

Spontaneous pneumothorax: primary (tall thin young men, apical blebs) and secondary (COPD, cystic lung disease — LAM, LCH, Birt-Hogg-Dubé, PCP, catamenial); on the erect film a visceral pleural line with no lung markings beyond it; tension is clinical with mediastinal shift and a depressed diaphragm. Pleural effusion: blunting of the costophrenic angle (~200 mL on PA, 50 mL on lateral), meniscus, subpulmonic effusion (apparent elevated hemidiaphragm with a lateral peak), and complete white-out with mediastinal shift away (effusion) or toward (collapse). CT attenuation: transudate near water, exudate/empyema with pleural thickening and enhancement, haemothorax >35 HU, chylothorax low. Ultrasound grades complexity (anechoic, septated, echogenic) and guides drainage. Malignant effusion: nodular pleural thickening, circumferential thickening >1 cm, mediastinal pleural involvement.

Pericardium

Pericardial effusion: an enlarged "water-bottle" cardiac silhouette with a normal lung pattern, an epicardial fat-pad (Oreo cookie) sign on the lateral, and on CT fluid >4 mm anterior to the heart; tamponade is an echo/clinical diagnosis but CT signs include a flattened or compressed right atrium/ventricle, dilated IVC/SVC and hepatic veins with contrast reflux, and a large effusion. Haemopericardium (>30–40 HU) after trauma, aortic dissection (type A rupturing into the pericardium), MI with ventricular rupture, or on anticoagulants. Pericarditis: thickened enhancing pericardium (>3–4 mm); constriction: calcified/thickened pericardium with a tubular ventricle and dilated atria. Pneumopericardium (contained by the pericardial reflection, does not rise above the pulmonary artery) vs pneumomediastinum (outlines the aorta and extends into the neck).

Mediastinum

Pneumomediastinum: linear lucencies outlining the mediastinal contours, the "continuous diaphragm" sign, the "V" sign of Naclerio at the lower thoracic aorta, and air tracking into the neck — spontaneous (asthma, cough, vomiting, inhaled drugs — Macklin effect, benign) versus secondary to oesophageal rupture (Boerhaave — left pleural effusion, mediastinal fluid, extraluminal contrast on CT oesophagram; a surgical emergency) or tracheobronchial injury. Acute mediastinitis: post-surgical or from oesophageal perforation or descending neck infection — mediastinal fat stranding, fluid, gas bubbles, and abscess; post-sternotomy air and fluid are normal for the first 2–3 weeks, so compare with baseline. Mediastinal haematoma (aortic, venous, spine fracture, line placement). Mediastinal masses presenting acutely: anterior masses compressing the airway or SVC (lymphoma, thymoma, germ cell — the "T" masses), a bleeding thymic cyst, oesophageal foreign bodies, and achalasia with aspiration.

Key points
  • Erect vs supine pneumothorax signs; secondary pneumothorax means cystic lung disease or COPD
  • Effusion >35 HU = blood; enhancing thick pleura = exudate/empyema
  • Haemopericardium: trauma, type A dissection, ventricular rupture
  • Pneumomediastinum + left effusion + vomiting = Boerhaave until excluded
  • Pneumopericardium stops at the pulmonary artery; pneumomediastinum goes to the neck
Never miss / red flags
  • Tension pneumothorax
  • Tamponade physiology on CT (flattened RA/RV, contrast reflux)
  • Boerhaave syndrome
  • SVC syndrome from an anterior mediastinal mass
  • Massive haemothorax
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Skin folds, tubes and the scapular edge mimicking a pleural line
  • Subpulmonic effusion read as a high diaphragm
  • Pericardial fat vs effusion (fat attenuation)
  • Post-sternotomy air within 3 weeks is expected

Acute aortic syndromes & the post-operative thoracic aorta

📖 Requisites pp. 255–263FY + Board
Protocol / technique
  • ECG-gated CTA of the whole aorta: non-contrast first (intramural haematoma, displaced intimal calcification, haemopericardium), arterial phase from the thoracic inlet to the femoral heads, delayed phase for the false lumen, endoleaks and the kidneys
  • Report the Stanford type, the entry tear and extent, true vs false lumen (true: smaller, surrounded by calcification, continuous with the undissected aorta, "beak" sign points into the false lumen, cobwebs in the false lumen), branch-vessel involvement (coronaries, arch vessels, coeliac, SMA, renals, iliacs), malperfusion, aortic valve/root, pericardial or pleural blood, and the maximal diameter

Aneurysm

Ascending >4 cm or descending >3 cm is aneurysmal; rupture risk rises sharply above 5.5–6 cm. Signs of rupture or impending rupture: rapid growth, periaortic haematoma or stranding, a high-attenuation crescent in the mural thrombus, a draped aorta over the spine, a focal contour bulge, and a mediastinal/pleural haematoma. Infectious (mycotic) aortitis: a saccular, lobulated, rapidly changing aneurysm with periaortic soft tissue, gas, and adjacent vertebral osteomyelitis, in a septic or immunocompromised patient.

Aortic dissection

Stanford A involves the ascending aorta (surgical; complications — rupture into the pericardium, coronary occlusion/MI, aortic regurgitation, arch-vessel stroke); Stanford B begins beyond the left subclavian (medical unless complicated by malperfusion, rupture, or rapid growth — then TEVAR). The intimal flap separates a true and a false lumen; the false lumen is usually larger with slower flow and may thrombose. Malperfusion is either static (the dissection extends into the branch, narrowing it) or dynamic (the flap intermittently occludes the ostium). Pitfalls: pulsation artefact in the ascending aorta on non-gated studies, streak from the SVC contrast bolus, and the aortic root at the sinuses.

Intramural haematoma & penetrating ulcer

Intramural haematoma (IMH): a crescentic high-attenuation (>60 HU) thickening of the aortic wall on the non-contrast series that does not enhance and displaces intimal calcification inward; classified A/B like dissection and managed similarly; it may resolve, progress to dissection, or form an ulcer-like projection. Penetrating atherosclerotic ulcer (PAU): an ulcer crater extending through the intima into the media, usually in the descending aorta of an elderly patient with heavy atherosclerosis, often with a localised IMH; risk of pseudoaneurysm and rupture. Together with dissection these are the acute aortic syndromes — clinically indistinguishable, so image the whole aorta.

Post-operative and post-stent aorta

Normal post-repair findings: a graft (slightly higher attenuation than the native wall, sometimes with a felt strip at the anastomoses), the "elephant trunk" dangling into the descending aorta, surgical clips, perigraft fluid or gas for a few weeks, and a wrapped native aorta around an interposition graft (inclusion technique — a perigraft space that fills with contrast can be normal). Complications: anastomotic pseudoaneurysm, graft infection (perigraft fluid/gas beyond 3 months, aorto-enteric/aorto-bronchial fistula), and residual dissection distal to the graft. Stent-grafts (TEVAR): endoleaks type I (attachment site — proximal Ia, distal Ib; needs treatment), II (retrograde branch filling — intercostals, left subclavian; often observed), III (component separation or fabric tear), IV (porosity), V (endotension); other complications — migration, kinking, stent collapse, infection, retrograde type A dissection, and spinal cord ischaemia. Delayed-phase images are essential for endoleak detection.

Key points
  • Non-contrast + arterial + delayed, ECG-gated, whole aorta
  • True lumen: smaller, calcified wall, beak sign points into the false lumen; cobwebs = false lumen
  • Type A = surgery; type B = medical unless complicated
  • IMH: crescentic >60 HU on non-contrast, no enhancement; PAU: crater beyond the intima
  • Endoleak I and III need repair; II usually watched
Never miss / red flags
  • Type A dissection with haemopericardium or coronary involvement
  • Rupture: periaortic haematoma, hyperdense crescent
  • Malperfusion of the SMA, renals, or a limb
  • Type I endoleak with sac enlargement
  • Mycotic aneurysm
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Pulsation artefact mimicking a type A flap
  • Aortic spindle, ductus diverticulum and infundibula
  • Contrast streak from the SVC/brachiocephalic vein
  • Thrombosed false lumen vs mural thrombus in an aneurysm (displaced calcification favours dissection)

Pulmonary embolism

📖 Requisites pp. 263–269FY + Board

Clinical framework

Pre-test probability (Wells, Geneva) and D-dimer select patients for CTPA; PERC rules out the lowest-risk group without testing. Risk factors: immobility, surgery, malignancy, pregnancy/oestrogen, thrombophilia, prior VTE. Mortality depends on right heart strain and haemodynamics: massive PE (hypotension) → thrombolysis/embolectomy; submassive (RV strain with normal BP) → risk stratification; low risk → anticoagulation.

CTPA findings

Acute PE: a central filling defect that is surrounded by contrast ("polo mint" on axial, "railway track" on longitudinal), abrupt vessel cut-off, a vessel expanded by clot, and an acute angle between the clot and the vessel wall; peripheral wedge-shaped consolidation or ground-glass (infarct; the "reverse halo" of an infarct with central lucency), oligaemia (Westermark), and an effusion. Chronic PE: eccentric mural thrombus with an obtuse angle, webs and bands, calcified thrombus, recanalisation, abrupt narrowing, and signs of chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (enlarged main PA >29–33 mm, mosaic perfusion, RV hypertrophy, bronchial artery hypertrophy). Right heart strain on CT: RV/LV diameter ratio >1.0 (axial or 4-chamber), interventricular septal flattening or bowing to the left, contrast reflux into the IVC and hepatic veins, and a dilated main pulmonary artery. Clot burden scores (Qanadli, Mastora) have limited prognostic value compared with the RV/LV ratio.

Technique, artefacts, and alternatives

Bolus-tracked scan triggered in the main PA at ~100–120 HU, thin collimation, shallow inspiration or end-expiratory breath-hold to prevent transient interruption of contrast (dense unopacified IVC blood mixing with the SVC bolus in deep inspiration); dual-energy iodine maps show perfusion defects. Artefacts: flow-related mixing in the veins and in the lower-lobe arteries, respiratory motion (double vessel contours), beam hardening from the SVC, partial-volume of lymph nodes at the hilum, mucus plugs in bronchi mimicking clots, and poor opacification in the pregnant or high-output patient. Report the level (main, lobar, segmental, subsegmental), laterality, chronicity, RV strain, and alternative diagnoses (pneumonia, oedema, dissection, pneumothorax — CTPA finds an alternative cause in ~25–30%). Ventilation/perfusion scintigraphy for pregnancy, contrast allergy, or renal failure; lower-limb Doppler for DVT; echocardiography for RV assessment; MR angiography selectively. In pregnancy, CTPA and V/Q both carry acceptable fetal dose; CTPA gives more breast dose, a perfusion-only scan with a normal CXR is a common compromise.

Key points
  • Polo mint / railway track = acute; eccentric obtuse-angled mural clot with webs = chronic
  • RV/LV >1.0 and septal bowing = RV strain — this decides management, so measure and state it
  • Infarct: peripheral wedge with a central lucent "reverse halo"
  • Breath-hold and trigger in the PA to avoid the IVC contrast interruption
  • Always list the alternative diagnosis when the CTPA is negative
Never miss / red flags
  • Saddle embolus or central bilateral clot with RV strain
  • Clot in transit in the right heart
  • Paradoxical embolus through a PFO (arterial emboli)
  • Occult malignancy or DVT extending to the IVC
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Mucus-filled bronchi and hilar nodes mimicking emboli
  • Flow artefact from unopacified blood in lower-lobe vessels (repeat with a delayed acquisition if needed)
  • Motion double contours mimicking flaps
  • Pulmonary artery sarcoma or tumour embolus (expands the vessel, enhances)

Coronary CT angiography & the triple rule-out in the emergency department

📖 Requisites pp. 269–280FY + Board

Chest pain triage

Coronary CTA is used for low-to-intermediate risk acute chest pain with a non-diagnostic ECG and negative initial troponin; a normal CCTA (no plaque, or <50% stenosis) allows safe discharge with a very high negative predictive value. It is not for high-risk or STEMI patients, who go to the catheter laboratory. Prerequisites: heart rate control (beta-blockers to ~60 bpm), sublingual nitroglycerin, sinus rhythm, breath-hold ability, and a manageable calcium burden; prospective ECG-triggered acquisition minimises dose.

Coronary anatomy

The right coronary artery arises from the right sinus and gives the conus branch, the SA nodal artery (~60%), acute marginals, and, in the right-dominant (85%) heart, the posterior descending artery and posterolateral branch from the crux. The left main arises from the left sinus and divides into the LAD (diagonals, septals) and the circumflex (obtuse marginals); a ramus intermedius trifurcation is common. Dominance is defined by which artery supplies the PDA (right 85%, left 8%, co-dominant 7%). The 18-segment SCCT model standardises reporting.

Coronary anomalies

Malignant anomalies are those with an interarterial course between the aorta and pulmonary artery — the left coronary from the right sinus (or the RCA from the left sinus) with a slit-like ostium and an acute-angle intramural course — associated with sudden death in young athletes; the retroaortic, prepulmonic and subpulmonic (septal) courses are benign. ALCAPA (anomalous left coronary from the pulmonary artery) presents in infancy with ischaemia and a dilated RCA with collaterals. Myocardial bridging (intramyocardial LAD segment) and coronary fistulae are usually benign. Ectasia and aneurysms (Kawasaki, atherosclerosis).

Image evaluation and reporting

Evaluate each segment on curved multiplanar reformats and cross-sections: plaque type (calcified, non-calcified, mixed — high-risk features are low attenuation <30 HU, positive remodelling, spotty calcification, and the napkin-ring sign), stenosis grade (CAD-RADS 0–5: 0 none, 1 minimal 1–24%, 2 mild 25–49%, 3 moderate 50–69%, 4 severe 70–99%, 5 occluded), plaque burden, and stents/grafts. Acute coronary syndrome findings: an intraluminal filling defect or occlusion, high-risk plaque, and a subendocardial or transmural perfusion defect (hypoenhancing myocardium on the arterial phase; delayed enhancement on late images). Non-coronary causes of chest pain seen on the same scan: dissection, PE, pericarditis, pneumonia, pneumothorax, oesophageal disease. The triple rule-out protocol (coronaries, aorta, pulmonary arteries in one ECG-gated acquisition with a dual-phase biphasic injection) is technically feasible but increases contrast and dose, and is reserved for the equivocal presentation.

Key points
  • CCTA for low/intermediate-risk chest pain with a negative troponin; a normal scan discharges
  • Dominance = who supplies the PDA; right in 85%
  • Interarterial course with a slit-like intramural origin = malignant anomaly
  • CAD-RADS 0–5; high-risk plaque: low attenuation, positive remodelling, spotty calcium, napkin ring
  • Look for the non-coronary causes on every chest-pain CT
Never miss / red flags
  • Left main or proximal LAD severe stenosis / thrombus
  • Interarterial anomalous coronary in a young patient with syncope
  • Myocardial hypoenhancement with a coronary occlusion
  • Type A dissection extending into a coronary ostium
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Motion and blooming artefact from calcium overestimating stenosis
  • Beam hardening from dense contrast in the right atrium
  • Stent evaluation limited below 3 mm
  • Myocardial bridging misread as stenosis

Bowel obstruction

📖 Requisites pp. 281–286FY + Board

Small-bowel obstruction

Causes in order: adhesions (60–75%), hernias (external — inguinal, femoral, incisional, obturator in elderly women; internal — paraduodenal, transmesenteric after Roux-en-Y), tumours, Crohn strictures, intussusception, gallstone ileus, bezoar, and volvulus. Radiograph: dilated small-bowel loops >3 cm with valvulae conniventes, air–fluid levels of differing heights, and a paucity of colonic gas; the "string of beads" sign with fluid-filled loops. CT: dilated loops (>2.5–3 cm) proximal to a transition point with collapsed loops beyond; the "small-bowel faeces" sign (particulate material in the dilated loop, marking the transition point); grade the obstruction (high vs low grade by the degree of collapse distally and passage of oral contrast) and identify the cause (a beak at an adhesion, a hernia sac, a mass, a wall-thickening stricture, a target of intussusception). Closed-loop obstruction (two transition points, a C- or U-shaped loop, radial mesenteric vessels converging on a "whirl", a beak at both ends, fixed position) is a surgical emergency because it strangulates. Signs of ischaemia/strangulation: bowel wall thickening or, worse, thinning, reduced or absent mural enhancement (compare loops; use the non-contrast series to exclude haemorrhage), mesenteric oedema and haziness, engorged mesenteric veins, ascites, pneumatosis and portal venous gas, and a "serrated beak". Gallstone ileus: Rigler triad — pneumobilia, an ectopic calcified gallstone (usually impacted at the terminal ileum), and small-bowel obstruction.

Large-bowel obstruction

Causes: colorectal carcinoma (60%; apple-core, shouldered stricture with a soft-tissue mass — obstructing sigmoid tumours are the commonest), sigmoid volvulus (elderly, constipated, institutionalised — a massively dilated ahaustral loop pointing to the right upper quadrant, "coffee bean", the "northern exit" of the apex above T10, and on CT the whirl of the mesocolon; decompress endoscopically), caecal volvulus (younger, mobile caecum — the dilated caecum in the left upper quadrant with the ileocaecal valve visible, a "comma" shape, and a decompressed distal colon; surgical), diverticular stricture, and hernia. The competent ileocaecal valve creates a closed loop: the caecum dilates most (>9 cm diameter is a perforation risk; >12–15 cm imminent); with an incompetent valve, the small bowel decompresses the colon. Pseudo-obstruction (Ogilvie): diffuse colonic dilatation without a transition point in the post-operative or medically ill patient; the caecal diameter still dictates the risk. Toxic megacolon: transverse colon >6 cm with a thick wall, loss of haustra, pseudopolyps, in fulminant colitis (UC, C. difficile, ischaemia) — a perforation risk that contraindicates enema and colonoscopy.

Key points
  • Transition point + small-bowel faeces sign; closed loop = two transition points, whirl, radial vessels
  • Ischaemia: reduced wall enhancement (check non-contrast), mesenteric oedema, ascites, pneumatosis
  • Sigmoid volvulus: coffee bean to the RUQ, ahaustral; caecal volvulus: LUQ, comma
  • Caecum >9 cm → perforation risk; toxic megacolon >6 cm transverse colon
  • Always identify the cause and the hernia orifices
Never miss / red flags
  • Closed-loop obstruction with ischaemia
  • Caecal volvulus
  • Caecal diameter >12 cm
  • Toxic megacolon
  • Free air, pneumatosis or portal venous gas in an obstructed patient
  • Obturator/femoral hernia in an elderly woman (easily missed on clinical examination)
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Ileus vs obstruction: ileus has diffuse dilatation of small and large bowel without a transition point
  • Gastric outlet vs small-bowel obstruction
  • Faeces sign in a chronically constipated patient without obstruction
  • Collapsed distal loops from an enema or recent evacuation

Infectious & inflammatory bowel disease

📖 Requisites pp. 286–296FY + Board

Appendicitis

The commonest surgical emergency. CT (with IV contrast; oral/rectal contrast optional): appendiceal diameter >6 mm (better: wall thickening >3 mm and mural enhancement), periappendiceal fat stranding, an appendicolith (30%), caecal apical thickening (the "arrowhead" sign of contrast pointing to the occluded orifice, the "caecal bar"), free fluid, and a normal appendix filled with air or contrast excludes it. Perforation: extraluminal air or appendicolith, abscess, phlegmon, a defect in the enhancing wall, and ileus; an abscess >3–4 cm is drained percutaneously with interval appendectomy. Ultrasound for children and pregnancy (non-compressible, >6 mm, hyperaemia); MRI in pregnancy (T2 high lumen, wall oedema, periappendiceal fluid — the appendix migrates superiorly in the third trimester). Mimics: mesenteric adenitis, terminal ileitis (Yersinia, Crohn), caecal diverticulitis, epiploic appendagitis, ovarian pathology, and a mucocele (a dilated fluid-filled appendix with wall calcification — do not perforate; pseudomyxoma risk). Tumours of the appendix in adults over 40 with a mass >15 mm or a soft-tissue component.

Diverticulitis and colitis

Diverticulitis: segmental wall thickening (>4 mm) with pericolic fat stranding centred on a diverticulum, engorged vasa recta ("comb" sign), fluid in the root of the mesocolon; complications — abscess (Hinchey II drained percutaneously), free perforation (Hinchey III/IV), fistula (colovesical — air in the bladder), and obstruction; a right-sided diverticulitis mimics appendicitis. Distinguishing diverticulitis from a perforated carcinoma: a long segment (>10 cm), diverticula, and preserved mucosal enhancement favour diverticulitis; a short shouldered segment, pericolic nodes >1 cm, and a mass favour carcinoma — colonoscopy after the acute episode. Colitis patterns: infectious (pancolitis or right-sided — Salmonella, Shigella, E. coli; segmental — Yersinia/TB ileocaecal), pseudomembranous (C. difficile — marked wall thickening, the "accordion" sign of contrast trapped between thickened folds, ascites, pancolitis, and toxic megacolon), ischaemic (watershed splenic flexure/rectosigmoid, elderly, thumbprinting, and pneumatosis in transmural infarction), ulcerative colitis (continuous from the rectum, no skip lesions, loss of haustra, "lead pipe" chronic), Crohn (skip lesions, terminal ileum, fistulae, abscess, creeping fat, comb sign, fibrofatty proliferation; acute flare shows mural stratification/target sign with hyperenhancing mucosa), neutropenic (typhlitis — caecum and ascending colon in the neutropenic patient; pneumatosis warrants surgery), radiation and stercoral (a large faecaloma with focal wall thickening/perforation of the rectosigmoid in the elderly). Epiploic appendagitis (a small pericolic fat-density oval with a hyperattenuating rim and central dot, next to a normal colon — conservative) and omental infarction (larger, right-sided, cake-like) are the "do not operate" mimics.

Upper GI and small-bowel inflammation

Peptic ulcer perforation: free air (small volume, under the liver, in the fissure for the ligamentum teres, periportal), fluid, and focal duodenal/gastric wall discontinuity or thickening; a walled-off perforation forms an abscess. Emphysematous gastritis (gas in the wall of a thick, ischaemic stomach — septic, high mortality) vs gastric emphysema (linear gas without wall thickening after instrumentation — benign). Infectious enteritis (viral, bacterial — diffuse wall thickening, fluid-filled loops, mesenteric nodes), coeliac crisis, angioedema of the bowel (ACE inhibitors — segmental submucosal oedema with ascites, resolves in days), and intramural haematoma (anticoagulants; a hyperdense, thick, spontaneously dense wall).

Key points
  • Appendicitis on CT: >6 mm, wall enhancement, stranding, arrowhead sign; abscess >3–4 cm → drainage
  • Diverticulitis vs carcinoma: long segment with diverticula vs short shouldered mass; scope afterward
  • C. difficile: accordion sign, pancolitis, ascites; typhlitis: right colon in neutropenia
  • Ischaemic colitis: watershed distribution in the elderly; pneumatosis = infarction
  • Epiploic appendagitis and omental infarction do not need surgery
Never miss / red flags
  • Perforated appendicitis/diverticulitis with free air and peritonitis
  • Toxic megacolon
  • Emphysematous gastritis
  • Bowel angioedema misdiagnosed as ischaemia (history!)
  • Appendiceal mucocele — do not aspirate
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Normal appendix >6 mm in a collapsed state; thin-walled fluid-filled appendix
  • Terminal ileitis mistaken for appendicitis
  • Pseudothickening of the collapsed colon
  • Faeces-filled caecum mimicking a mass

Mesenteric ischaemia & vascular bowel disease

📖 Requisites pp. 296–302FY + Board

Acute mesenteric ischaemia has four causes: SMA embolism (~50%; atrial fibrillation, cardiac thrombus — the embolus lodges beyond the middle colic origin so the proximal jejunum and transverse colon are often spared), SMA thrombosis (25%; on pre-existing atherosclerosis at the origin, with a history of intestinal angina and weight loss), non-occlusive mesenteric ischaemia (NOMI; low-flow states, vasopressors, dialysis — patent but diffusely narrowed mesenteric arteries with alternating segments of spasm and dilatation, and diffuse bowel changes), and mesenteric venous thrombosis (10%; hypercoagulable, portal hypertension, pancreatitis — SMV/portal filling defect with wall thickening from congestion, mesenteric oedema and ascites; the most survivable form, treated with anticoagulation).

Protocol / technique
  • Biphasic CT: non-contrast (hyperdense haemorrhagic wall, calcification), arterial phase (SMA/SMV occlusion, embolus, thrombus), portal venous phase (mural enhancement, venous thrombosis, free fluid); no positive oral contrast, which obscures mural enhancement; thin sections with sagittal MIPs of the SMA origin

Bowel findings

Early: wall thickening (venous ischaemia and reperfusion) or paradoxically a thin, paper-like wall (arterial ischaemia without reperfusion), reduced or absent mural enhancement (the most specific sign; compare with adjacent loops on the venous phase), mesenteric fat stranding and fluid, dilated fluid-filled loops (ileus of the ischaemic segment), and hyperenhancing mucosa in reperfusion or venous congestion. Late (transmural infarction): pneumatosis intestinalis (linear/curvilinear gas in the wall — bubbly gas in a thick wall is worse than linear gas in a dilated benign loop), mesenteric and portal venous gas (branching gas to the periphery of the liver, unlike central pneumobilia), free air and peritonitis. Ischaemic colitis in the watershed areas is usually non-occlusive and self-limited; a right-sided ischaemic colitis suggests SMA disease and a worse prognosis. Chronic mesenteric ischaemia: high-grade stenosis of two of the three mesenteric arteries with collaterals (arc of Riolan, marginal artery), post-prandial pain and weight loss. Other vascular bowel disease: median arcuate ligament compression of the coeliac axis, SMA syndrome (duodenum compressed between the SMA and aorta in thin patients — an aortomesenteric angle <22° and distance <8 mm), vasculitis (polyarteritis nodosa, lupus — long-segment thickening with target sign in a young patient), and radiation enteritis.

Key points
  • Embolus spares the proximal jejunum and transverse colon; thrombosis is at the origin; NOMI is diffuse; venous thrombosis thickens the wall and is treated with anticoagulation
  • Absent mural enhancement is the key sign; use the non-contrast series
  • Pneumatosis + portal venous gas = transmural infarction — but pneumatosis alone can be benign
  • No positive oral contrast for ischaemia protocols
Never miss / red flags
  • SMA embolus or thrombus — surgical/endovascular emergency
  • Portal venous gas with pneumatosis
  • Non-enhancing bowel segment
  • Closed-loop obstruction (the commonest surgical cause of ischaemia)
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Benign pneumatosis (COPD, steroids, post-endoscopy, scleroderma) — no wall thickening, no fluid, well patient
  • Pneumobilia (central) vs portal venous gas (peripheral)
  • Dilated loops with reduced enhancement from an inadequate contrast bolus
  • Bowel angioedema and intramural haematoma mimicking venous ischaemia

Pancreaticobiliary emergencies

📖 Requisites pp. 297–302FY + Board

Acute pancreatitis

Diagnosis is clinical (two of: pain, lipase >3× normal, imaging). CT is for severity and complications after 48–72 hours, not for diagnosis — an early scan underestimates necrosis. The Revised Atlanta classification: interstitial oedematous pancreatitis (enlarged, homogeneously enhancing gland with peripancreatic fat stranding/fluid) versus necrotising pancreatitis (non-enhancing parenchyma <30 HU on the portal venous phase, ± peripancreatic necrosis). Collections: acute peripancreatic fluid collection (<4 weeks, no wall, oedematous pancreatitis) → pseudocyst (>4 weeks, encapsulated, homogeneous fluid); acute necrotic collection (<4 weeks, heterogeneous, necrotising) → walled-off necrosis (>4 weeks, encapsulated with solid debris). Infected necrosis: gas within the collection (or clinical/aspiration) — drained/necrosectomy. Vascular complications: splenic vein thrombosis (gastric varices), pseudoaneurysm (splenic, gastroduodenal — a contrast-density focus within a collection, bleeding risk), and haemorrhage. Other: bowel ischaemia, abdominal compartment syndrome, and disconnected duct syndrome. The CT severity index (Balthazar grade A–E plus necrosis percentage) and modified CTSI are used for reporting; gallstones (ultrasound) and alcohol are the main causes, then hypertriglyceridaemia, ERCP, drugs, and autoimmune. Chronic pancreatitis presenting acutely: calcifications, ductal dilatation and strictures, atrophy, pseudocysts — differentiate a pseudocyst from a cystic neoplasm and a mass-forming pancreatitis from carcinoma (duct penetrating sign favours inflammation).

Gallstones and cholecystitis

Ultrasound is first line: stones (echogenic, mobile, shadowing), gallbladder wall >3 mm, pericholecystic fluid, a distended gallbladder (>4 cm transverse), the sonographic Murphy sign (the most useful single sign), and a hyperaemic wall. CT is done when the diagnosis is unclear or complications are suspected: wall thickening and enhancement, pericholecystic fat stranding, transient hepatic hyperenhancement adjacent to the gallbladder, and the complications — gangrenous cholecystitis (irregular, absent wall enhancement, intraluminal membranes, asymmetric wall thickening; high perforation rate), emphysematous cholecystitis (gas in the wall or lumen, diabetic elderly men, clostridia — urgent cholecystectomy), perforation (wall defect, pericholecystic abscess, bilioma), gallbladder empyema, and Mirizzi syndrome (a stone in the cystic duct/neck compressing the common hepatic duct). Acalculous cholecystitis in the ICU/post-operative patient (thick wall, sludge, distension, no stones — HIDA non-filling or percutaneous cholecystostomy). Choledocholithiasis: a dilated CBD (>6 mm, or >8 mm post-cholecystectomy/elderly) with an intraductal echogenic/hyperdense (on CT, only ~70% are visible) focus, the "target" or "crescent" sign of a stone surrounded by bile; MRCP is the confirmatory test before ERCP. Ascending cholangitis (Charcot triad; periportal oedema, heterogeneous hepatic enhancement, ductal dilatation with wall thickening and enhancement, pneumobilia if instrumented) — a drainage emergency. Hepatolithiasis (intrahepatic stones — recurrent pyogenic cholangitis in East Asia).

Key points
  • Pancreatitis CT at 48–72 h for necrosis; Atlanta: APFC → pseudocyst, ANC → WON; gas in a collection = infection
  • Pseudoaneurysm within a peripancreatic collection is a bleeding risk — arterial phase
  • Sonographic Murphy sign + stones + thick wall = cholecystitis; absent wall enhancement = gangrene; gas = emphysematous
  • CBD >6 mm (+1 mm/decade over 60; up to 10 mm post-cholecystectomy); MRCP before ERCP
  • Cholangitis is a drainage emergency
Never miss / red flags
  • Infected necrosis
  • Pseudoaneurysm/haemorrhage in pancreatitis
  • Gangrenous or emphysematous cholecystitis, perforation
  • Cholangitis with obstruction
  • Mass-forming lesion in "pancreatitis" without a cause
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Contracted normal gallbladder wall appears thick after a meal
  • Wall thickening from hepatitis, heart failure, hypoalbuminaemia, cirrhosis without cholecystitis
  • Sludge and polyps vs stones
  • Early CT underestimating necrosis; unopacified splenic vein mimicking thrombosis

Liver & spleen emergencies

📖 Requisites pp. 302–309FY + Board

Focal hepatic infection

Pyogenic abscess: from biliary sepsis, portal pyaemia (appendicitis, diverticulitis), haematogenous seeding, or direct spread; a unilocular or multiloculated "cluster" of low-density lesions with rim enhancement and perilesional oedema/transient hyperenhancement, and gas in ~20% (Klebsiella in diabetics, gas-forming); ultrasound shows a complex hypoechoic collection; treatment is percutaneous drainage plus antibiotics. Amoebic abscess: a solitary right-lobe lesion in a patient from an endemic area, "anchovy paste" — aspiration is usually unnecessary (serology). Hydatid cyst: daughter cysts, the water-lily sign of a detached membrane, calcified wall — rupture into the biliary tree or peritoneum causes anaphylaxis; do not aspirate without preparation. Fungal microabscesses (Candida): multiple tiny lesions in the liver and spleen in the recovering neutropenic patient. Hepatic infarction is rare (dual supply) except after transplantation or with hepatic artery thrombosis; peliosis and HELLP haematomas in pregnancy.

Hepatitis and diffuse disease

Acute hepatitis: hepatomegaly, periportal oedema, a "starry sky" on ultrasound, gallbladder wall thickening without cholecystitis; imaging excludes obstruction. Alcoholic hepatitis and hepatic steatosis (attenuation <40 HU or >10 HU less than spleen); Budd-Chiari (hepatic vein/IVC thrombosis with a "nutmeg" or "flip-flop" enhancement pattern, caudate hypertrophy, and ascites); acute portal vein thrombosis (bland vs tumour thrombus that enhances and expands the vein); hepatic congestion in right heart failure; spontaneous rupture of a liver lesion (hepatocellular carcinoma, adenoma in a young woman on oral contraceptives, haemangioma rarely) — haemoperitoneum with a sentinel clot around the liver and a heterogeneous mass with active extravasation.

Spleen

Splenic abscess (endocarditis, IV drug use, immunocompromise — low-density lesion with a rim, gas), splenic infarction (a peripheral wedge-shaped non-enhancing area, or a globally non-enhancing spleen in splenic artery embolism/torsion; emboli from AF, endocarditis, sickle cell autoinfarction, haematological malignancy with massive splenomegaly, pancreatitis with splenic vein thrombosis), splenic torsion (wandering spleen — an ectopic spleen with a whirl of the vascular pedicle and non-enhancement), spontaneous splenic rupture (infectious mononucleosis, malaria, malignancy, anticoagulation), and splenic vein thrombosis. Report splenomegaly (>13 cm) and the differential (portal hypertension, lymphoma/leukaemia, infection, storage disease).

Key points
  • Cluster sign with rim enhancement and perilesional hyperenhancement = pyogenic abscess → drain
  • Gas-containing abscess: Klebsiella in diabetics; microabscesses in the liver and spleen: fungal
  • Hydatid: do not aspirate blindly; amoebic: serology and treat
  • Spontaneous haemoperitoneum around the liver in a young woman → adenoma; in cirrhosis → HCC
  • Wedge-shaped non-enhancing spleen = infarct; whirl + non-enhancement = torsion
Never miss / red flags
  • Ruptured hepatic lesion with active bleeding
  • Gas-forming liver abscess in a septic patient
  • Budd-Chiari with fulminant failure
  • Splenic abscess in endocarditis
  • Complete splenic infarction / torsion
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Necrotic metastasis or cystic tumour mimicking abscess (no rim hyperaemia, no gas)
  • Transient hepatic attenuation differences from arterioportal shunting
  • Focal steatosis/sparing near the falciform ligament and gallbladder fossa
  • Splenic clefts and accessory spleens

Non-traumatic genitourinary emergencies

📖 Requisites pp. 305–309FY + Board

Nephrolithiasis

Non-contrast low-dose CT is the standard for renal colic (>95% sensitive): a hyperdense calculus in the ureter with proximal hydroureteronephrosis, perinephric and periureteric stranding, the "soft-tissue rim" sign (oedematous ureteric wall around a stone — distinguishes it from a phlebolith, which has a "comet-tail" of the vein and a lucent centre), unilateral renal enlargement and decreased density (oedema), and asymmetric perinephric fluid. Report the stone size (in the axial and coronal plane), location (UPJ, pelvic brim/iliac vessels, UVJ — the three narrowings), density (uric acid <500 HU — dissolvable; struvite/cystine; calcium oxalate >1000 HU — less responsive to lithotripsy), the number of stones, and complications — forniceal rupture (perinephric urine), pyonephrosis, a solitary kidney, and a stone-free ureter with residual dilatation (recent passage). Stones <5 mm pass spontaneously in ~90%; >10 mm rarely. Ultrasound in pregnancy and children: hydronephrosis, twinkling artefact, absent ureteric jet. Alternative diagnoses in the negative renal colic CT: appendicitis, diverticulitis, ovarian torsion, ruptured aneurysm, and renal infarct.

Upper urinary tract infection

Uncomplicated pyelonephritis is a clinical diagnosis; imaging is for the non-responder at 48–72 h, diabetics, the immunocompromised, and suspected obstruction. CT: striated/wedge-shaped hypoenhancing nephrogram (nephrographic phase 90–100 s), renal enlargement, perinephric stranding, thickening of Gerota fascia and the pelvicalyceal wall, and a delayed persistent nephrogram in the affected zones. Renal abscess: a low-density non-enhancing collection with a thick rim; drained if >3 cm or unresponsive. Emphysematous pyelonephritis: gas within the renal parenchyma (class 1–4 by Huang and Tseng) in a diabetic — a nephrectomy/drainage emergency with high mortality; gas confined to the collecting system is emphysematous pyelitis, less severe. Pyonephrosis: an obstructed, infected system (echogenic debris and fluid–fluid level in the dilated pelvis, wall thickening) — urgent nephrostomy. Xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis: a non-functioning enlarged kidney replaced by low-density (lipid-laden) masses around a staghorn calculus ("bear paw" sign), with perinephric extension and fistulae — nephrectomy; mimics tumour. Emphysematous cystitis: gas in the bladder wall in a diabetic woman — antibiotics.

Renal vascular emergencies

Renal infarct: a wedge-shaped non-enhancing cortical area with a "cortical rim" sign of preserved capsular enhancement (from capsular collaterals — also seen in renal vein thrombosis and acute tubular necrosis, and absent in pyelonephritis); global infarction from renal artery occlusion (embolus, dissection, trauma). Renal vein thrombosis: an enlarged kidney with a delayed/persistent nephrogram, a filling defect in the renal vein extending to the IVC, and perinephric stranding — nephrotic syndrome, malignancy (tumour thrombus in RCC), dehydration in neonates. Spontaneous renal haemorrhage (Wunderlich): a perinephric haematoma from an angiomyolipoma (>4 cm, fat-containing), RCC, vasculitis, or anticoagulation — follow-up CT after the haematoma resolves to find the underlying mass. Renal papillary necrosis (diabetes, NSAIDs, sickle cell) presenting with colic from a sloughed papilla.

Key points
  • Soft-tissue rim sign = ureteric stone; comet-tail = phlebolith; report size, site, density
  • Striated nephrogram = pyelonephritis; parenchymal gas = emphysematous pyelonephritis (drain/nephrectomy); obstructed infected system = nephrostomy
  • Cortical rim sign = infarct or RVT, not infection
  • Wunderlich: find the AML or RCC on follow-up
  • XGP: bear paw around a staghorn
Never miss / red flags
  • Pyonephrosis
  • Emphysematous pyelonephritis
  • Obstructing stone in a solitary or transplanted kidney
  • Renal artery occlusion
  • Perinephric haematoma with active bleeding
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Phleboliths and arterial calcification vs distal ureteric stones
  • Physiological hydronephrosis of pregnancy (right > left)
  • Post-passage residual dilatation without a stone
  • Extrarenal pelvis mimicking hydronephrosis

Emergent abdominal findings: pneumoperitoneum, haemoperitoneum, retroperitoneal haemorrhage, obstruction

📖 Requisites pp. 309–315FY + Board

Pneumoperitoneum

Erect chest radiograph detects as little as 1–2 mL of free air under the diaphragm; the left lateral decubitus is the alternative; supine signs — Rigler (both sides of the bowel wall visible), the football sign (a large central lucency), the falciform ligament and urachus outlined, the "cupola" sign under the central diaphragm, the "continuous diaphragm", triangular lucencies between loops, and air over the liver (the "hyperlucent liver"). CT with lung windows is far more sensitive: air anterior to the liver, in the fissure for the ligamentum teres, around the porta, in the lesser sac, between leaves of the mesentery (a duodenal/retroperitoneal perforation gives air around the right kidney, duodenum and psoas). Localising the perforation: the site with the largest volume of adjacent air, wall discontinuity, focal wall thickening, and adjacent fat stranding; proximal perforations (peptic ulcer) give large volumes of free air; colonic perforations give faecal contamination and abscess; appendiceal and diverticular perforations give small amounts of localised air. Non-surgical causes: post-laparotomy/laparoscopy (resolves in 1–2 weeks, may persist longer), peritoneal dialysis, pneumomediastinum tracking down, pneumatosis cystoides, ventilation, and a ruptured tubo-ovarian/vaginal insufflation source.

Spontaneous haemoperitoneum

Non-traumatic causes: ruptured ectopic pregnancy and haemorrhagic ovarian cyst (the commonest in women of reproductive age), ruptured hepatic tumour (HCC, adenoma), splenic rupture (mononucleosis, malaria, lymphoma), ruptured aneurysm/pseudoaneurysm (splenic artery in pregnancy, visceral aneurysms, SMA in segmental arterial mediolysis), anticoagulation (rectus sheath and mesenteric haematoma), and post-procedural (biopsy, ablation). Blood in the peritoneum measures 30–45 HU (higher with clotting); the sentinel clot points to the source; active extravasation and pseudoaneurysm on arterial and delayed phases direct embolisation. Haemoperitoneum in a young woman with a positive pregnancy test is an ectopic pregnancy until proven otherwise.

Spontaneous retroperitoneal haemorrhage

Anticoagulation (iliopsoas and rectus sheath haematomas — a hyperdense mass with the "haematocrit" fluid–fluid level of unclotted blood in anticoagulated patients), ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm (the most important: a large periaortic haematoma extending into the retroperitoneum with a disrupted aortic wall, a hyperdense crescent in the mural thrombus, a draped aorta over the vertebra, or the "tangential calcium" sign — unstable patients bypass CT for theatre), renal (AML, RCC), adrenal haemorrhage (stress, sepsis, anticoagulation, antiphospholipid syndrome — bilateral in the septic or post-operative patient with hypotension), and psoas haematoma from a femoral puncture. Rectus sheath haematoma below the arcuate line spreads into the prevesical space and mimics a pelvic mass.

Biliary and urinary obstruction

Biliary obstruction: dilated intrahepatic ducts (>2 mm, the "double-barrel shotgun"/parallel channel sign), CBD >6 mm, and a level and cause — stone, stricture, pancreatic head mass (double-duct sign), cholangiocarcinoma (Klatskin at the confluence), ampullary tumour, Mirizzi, or extrinsic nodes; ultrasound first, CT for the cause, MRCP for the map, ERCP/PTC for drainage. Urinary obstruction: bilateral hydronephrosis with a distended bladder (outlet — prostate, urethral stricture, neurogenic bladder, clot retention); bilateral hydronephrosis with an empty bladder (bilateral ureteric obstruction — retroperitoneal fibrosis with medial deviation of the ureters, pelvic malignancy, lymphadenopathy); unilateral (stone, tumour, stricture, UPJ). Obstruction in a transplant or a solitary kidney is an emergency; obstruction with infection is a nephrostomy.

Key points
  • Erect CXR for free air; CT lung windows and the fissure for the ligamentum teres are where small volumes hide
  • Largest adjacent air + wall defect + stranding localises the perforation; retroperitoneal air = duodenum/posterior colon
  • Sentinel clot and HU point to the source of haemoperitoneum; young woman + haemoperitoneum = ectopic
  • Ruptured AAA: hyperdense crescent, draped aorta, discontinuous calcification
  • Double-duct sign = pancreatic head/ampullary tumour; infected obstruction = drain now
Never miss / red flags
  • Free intraperitoneal air with peritonitis
  • Ruptured AAA
  • Ruptured ectopic pregnancy
  • Bilateral adrenal haemorrhage
  • Obstructed, infected kidney or biliary tree
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Chilaiditi (colon interposed between the liver and the diaphragm) mimicking free air
  • Subdiaphragmatic fat and the curvilinear diaphragm muscle slips
  • Post-operative free air lasting up to 2–3 weeks
  • Physiological dilatation of the CBD after cholecystectomy and with age

Female pelvic emergencies: adnexa, uterus, tubes & pregnancy

📖 Requisites pp. 319–326FY + Board

Transabdominal then transvaginal ultrasound, with the serum β-hCG in hand for every woman of reproductive age with pelvic pain; CT is reserved for the non-gynaecological differential (appendicitis, diverticulitis, colic) and MRI for problem-solving in pregnancy.

Ovary

Haemorrhagic ovarian cyst: a unilocular cyst with fine reticular "fishnet" echoes or a retracting clot with concave margins, no internal flow, a "ring of fire" of peripheral flow, and often free fluid (rupture — haemoperitoneum in a young woman with a negative hCG); resolves within 6–8 weeks (follow-up at 6–12 weeks if >5 cm). Ovarian torsion: an enlarged ovary (>4 cm) with peripheral follicles and stromal oedema, a twisted pedicle ("whirlpool"), a midline or abnormally positioned ovary, free fluid, and variable Doppler — venous flow is lost first, arterial flow may persist because of the dual supply from the ovarian and uterine arteries; a lead-point mass (dermoid, cyst) in half; CT shows the enlarged ovary, deviation of the uterus to the affected side, and the twisted pedicle. Ruptured or torted dermoid (fat, fluid–fat level, calcification), and ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (bilateral massively enlarged multicystic ovaries, ascites, effusions after fertility treatment).

Tubes and uterus

Pelvic inflammatory disease progresses from endometritis (thick heterogeneous endometrium, fluid) through salpingitis (thick-walled tube) to pyosalpinx (a dilated tubular structure with echogenic contents and the "cogwheel" sign of thickened endosalpingeal folds on cross-section) and tubo-ovarian abscess (a complex multiloculated adnexal mass incorporating the ovary, thick enhancing walls, loss of the fat planes, free fluid; Fitz-Hugh-Curtis perihepatitis with capsular enhancement on CT). Hydrosalpinx (anechoic tube, "waist" sign, incomplete septa) is the chronic form. Endometriosis presenting acutely: rupture of an endometrioma (ground-glass echoes; T1 bright/T2 shading on MRI), or bowel/ureteric obstruction. Endometritis post-partum/post-instrumentation: thickened endometrium with fluid and gas (gas can be normal for up to 3 weeks after delivery). Dysfunctional uterine bleeding and fibroid degeneration (red degeneration in pregnancy — a painful fibroid with peripheral T1 high signal), a prolapsing submucosal fibroid, and a retained products of conception (endometrial mass with flow after delivery/miscarriage).

Pregnancy

Ectopic pregnancy: the discriminatory zone — an intrauterine gestational sac should be seen transvaginally when β-hCG is >1,500–3,000 IU/L; no intrauterine pregnancy plus an adnexal mass separate from the ovary (a "tubal ring" with an echogenic wall, a yolk sac or embryo, or an inhomogeneous adnexal mass), a "ring of fire" (non-specific — the corpus luteum has one too, but the corpus luteum moves with the ovary), free fluid (echogenic = blood; any fluid in Morison pouch = significant haemoperitoneum), and a "pseudogestational sac" (central, irregular fluid in the endometrial cavity, no yolk sac, no double decidual sign) that must not be mistaken for an intrauterine pregnancy. Sites: ampullary (most), isthmic, interstitial/cornual (a sac eccentric to the endometrium with a thin <5 mm myometrial mantle and the "interstitial line" sign — ruptures late and bleeds catastrophically), cervical, caesarean-scar, ovarian, abdominal, and heterotopic (IVF). Miscarriage criteria: a CRL ≥7 mm without a heartbeat, a mean sac diameter ≥25 mm without an embryo, or absence of an embryo with a heartbeat ≥2 weeks after a sac without a yolk sac (or ≥11 days after a sac with a yolk sac). Subchorionic haemorrhage, retained products, and molar pregnancy ("snowstorm"/vesicular endometrial mass with very high hCG and theca lutein cysts). Postpartum bleeding: retained products (an echogenic endometrial mass with flow), uterine atony, uterine AVM/pseudoaneurysm (high-velocity turbulent flow in the myometrium — embolise; do not curette), and haematoma. Ovarian vein thrombosis: postpartum fever and flank pain — a tubular low-density structure with an enhancing wall extending from the adnexa to the IVC on CT (right > left); anticoagulate and antibiotics. Placental abruption (a retroplacental haematoma that is iso/hypoechoic and easily missed; the diagnosis is clinical) and uterine rupture.

Key points
  • Every reproductive-age woman: β-hCG before the scan
  • No IUP above the discriminatory zone + adnexal mass/free fluid = ectopic; corpus luteum moves with the ovary, the tubal ring does not
  • Interstitial ectopic: eccentric sac, myometrial mantle <5 mm
  • Haemorrhagic cyst: fishnet/retracting clot, no internal flow, resolves
  • Torsion: big oedematous ovary with peripheral follicles; flow may be present
  • TOA: complex multiloculated mass with thick walls; cogwheel tube = pyosalpinx
Never miss / red flags
  • Ruptured ectopic with haemoperitoneum
  • Interstitial or caesarean-scar ectopic
  • Ovarian torsion
  • Tubo-ovarian abscess with rupture
  • Uterine AVM in postpartum bleeding — no curettage
  • Ovarian vein thrombosis extending to the IVC

  • Pseudogestational sac vs true IUP (double decidual sac sign, yolk sac)
  • Corpus luteum vs tubal ring
  • Physiological free fluid in the cul-de-sac
  • Dermoid with a "tip of the iceberg" sign mimicking bowel gas
  • Normal postpartum uterus with gas and heterogeneous endometrium

Arterial emergencies of the neck

📖 Requisites pp. 327–347FY + Board
Protocol / technique
  • Neck CTA: arch to circle of Willis, bolus-tracked in the aortic arch, sub-mm collimation, MIPs and curved reformats; include the vertebral artery origins and the V3/V4 segments
  • Ultrasound duplex for stenosis surveillance and first-line in some centres for dissection; MRI/MRA with fat-saturated T1 for intramural haematoma; catheter angiography for treatment

Penetrating neck injury

Zone I (clavicle to cricoid — great vessels, lung apex, oesophagus, trachea), zone II (cricoid to angle of the mandible — carotid and jugular, most accessible), zone III (above the angle — distal ICA, vertebral, pharynx; hard to explore surgically). "Hard signs" (active bleeding, expanding haematoma, bruit/thrill, pulse deficit, neurological deficit, airway compromise) go to theatre; everyone else gets CTA, which maps the wound tract and identifies vascular injury (pseudoaneurysm, occlusion, AV fistula, intimal flap, extravasation), and aerodigestive injury (air outside the airway, oesophageal wall thickening or extraluminal contrast on a swallow study). A tract passing within ~5 mm of a vessel with streak artefact from fragments may require conventional angiography. Venous injury (IJV) is under-recognised: an irregular, compressed or thrombosed IJV with perivascular haematoma.

Blunt cerebrovascular injury

Screening criteria (expanded Denver): high-energy mechanism with a Le Fort II/III, basilar skull fracture involving the carotid canal, cervical spine fracture (transverse foramen, C1–C3, subluxation), diffuse axonal injury with GCS <6, near-hanging with anoxia, seat-belt sign with swelling or altered mental status, and an unexplained neurological deficit or infarct. Biffl grades I–V (intimal irregularity <25%, dissection/haematoma ≥25%, pseudoaneurysm, occlusion, transection with extravasation); treatment is antithrombotic for I–II, endovascular for III and symptomatic lesions. Stroke rates rise with grade; the risk window is the first 72 hours, hence early screening.

Spontaneous dissection, stenosis, and other

Spontaneous carotid dissection: young to middle-aged patient with neck pain, headache, Horner syndrome (sympathetic fibres on the ICA) and ischaemic symptoms; CTA shows a tapered "flame" or "string" narrowing beginning 2–3 cm above the bifurcation and sparing the bulb (atheroma involves the bulb), an eccentric crescentic mural thickening, an increased external diameter of the vessel, a pseudoaneurysm, or an intimal flap; MRI T1 fat-saturated shows the crescent of methaemoglobin in the wall. Vertebral dissection favours V2–V3 at the level of C1–C2 and mimics a migraine — look at the vertebrals on every young stroke CTA. Fibromuscular dysplasia ("string of beads" in the mid-distal ICA) predisposes to dissection. Symptomatic carotid stenosis: NASCET measurement (residual lumen vs normal distal ICA), 70–99% symptomatic → endarterectomy within 2 weeks; the "string sign" of near-occlusion; free-floating thrombus; ulcerated plaque; and carotid occlusion with an ipsilateral watershed infarct. Carotid blowout in the irradiated head and neck cancer patient (pseudoaneurysm, extravasation — endovascular covered stent or occlusion). Carotid body tumour and other pulsatile masses; Takayasu and giant-cell arteritis (long, smooth wall thickening and enhancement — steroids, not stents); Eagle syndrome (an elongated styloid compressing the ICA); vertebrobasilar insufficiency and subclavian steal (reversed vertebral flow on Doppler with a proximal subclavian stenosis).

Key points
  • Hard signs → theatre; everything else → CTA; zones I and III are the surgically difficult ones
  • Denver criteria trigger a screening CTA; Biffl grade drives treatment; stroke risk peaks in the first 72 h
  • Dissection spares the bulb and begins 2–3 cm above it; atheroma lives at the bulb
  • Horner + neck pain + young stroke = carotid dissection; posterior circulation stroke in a young patient = vertebral dissection
  • NASCET 70–99% symptomatic → surgery in 2 weeks
Never miss / red flags
  • Active extravasation or expanding haematoma in the neck
  • Biffl grade III–V injury
  • Free-floating carotid thrombus
  • Carotid blowout
  • Bilateral or basilar-extending vertebral dissection
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Vessel loops and tortuosity mimicking dissection
  • Carotid web (a shelf-like intimal projection at the posterior bulb — a real stroke cause, but not dissection)
  • Mixing artefact in the IJV mimicking thrombus
  • Hypoplastic vertebral artery (normal variant, ends in PICA) vs occlusion

Mesenteric, visceral & aortic arterial emergencies

📖 Requisites pp. 347–353FY + Board

Abdominal aortic aneurysm

Infrarenal in 90%; >3 cm is aneurysmal; rupture risk is ~1%/year at 4–5 cm, rising to >10%/year above 6 cm; women rupture at smaller diameters. Signs of rupture (unstable patients skip the CT): retroperitoneal haematoma around the aorta and psoas extending into the pararenal spaces, active extravasation, a discontinuous calcified wall, the "draped aorta" over the vertebra (contained rupture), and the impending-rupture signs — a hyperattenuating crescent within the mural thrombus (acute haemorrhage dissecting into the thrombus), a focal bulge or bleb, "tangential calcium", and an increase in size on serial imaging. Aorto-caval fistula (early contrast in the IVC, high-output failure) and aorto-enteric fistula (duodenal — usually a graft complication: perigraft gas, loss of the fat plane between the graft and the third part of the duodenum, GI bleeding). Post-EVAR: endoleaks (I–V as in the thoracic aorta), limb occlusion, migration, infection. Inflammatory aneurysm (a thick enhancing rind with adherent duodenum/ureters — differentiate from contained rupture) and mycotic aneurysm (saccular, lobulated, periaortic stranding and gas, vertebral osteomyelitis). Aortic dissection extending from the thorax with visceral malperfusion; isolated abdominal aortic dissection; acute aortic occlusion (saddle embolus at the bifurcation — Leriche presents chronically) with bilateral limb ischaemia.

Visceral and mesenteric arteries

Acute mesenteric ischaemia is covered under bowel disease; the vascular side: SMA embolus (a filling defect beyond the middle colic origin), SMA thrombosis (calcified plaque at the origin with occlusion), coeliac/SMA dissection (isolated spontaneous dissection of the SMA in a middle-aged man — anticoagulation vs stenting), SMV thrombosis, and NOMI (diffusely narrowed, "string of sausages" mesenteric branches with poor bowel enhancement). Visceral artery aneurysms: splenic (the most common; rupture in the third trimester of pregnancy with high mortality; calcified ring in the left upper quadrant), hepatic (trauma, pancreatitis, post-procedure — haemobilia), SMA (mycotic, endocarditis), renal, gastroduodenal/pancreaticoduodenal (with coeliac stenosis), and pseudoaneurysms in pancreatitis. Segmental arterial mediolysis: a non-inflammatory arteriopathy with dissecting aneurysms of the mesenteric arteries in middle-aged patients presenting with spontaneous haemoperitoneum. Renal artery embolism/infarction, renal artery dissection (fibromuscular dysplasia), and renal AVM/AVF with haematuria.

GI bleeding

CT angiography (non-contrast, arterial, portal venous — no oral contrast) detects bleeding at ~0.3–0.5 mL/min: hyperdense contrast within the lumen on the arterial phase that changes on the delayed phase, plus the cause (diverticulum, angiodysplasia — a tuft of vessels with an early draining vein; tumour; ulcer; varices; Dieulafoy; aorto-enteric fistula). Scintigraphy (tagged red cells) detects slower bleeding (0.1 mL/min) and intermittent bleeding over 24 h, but localises poorly; catheter angiography (≥0.5–1 mL/min) is therapeutic — superselective embolisation. Upper GI bleeding is endoscopy first; lower GI bleeding is CTA first in the haemodynamically significant patient. Haemobilia (blood in the biliary tree after a liver procedure — hepatic artery pseudoaneurysm) and haemosuccus pancreaticus (splenic artery pseudoaneurysm into the pancreatic duct).

Key points
  • Hyperdense crescent, draped aorta, discontinuous calcium = rupturing AAA; unstable = theatre without CT
  • Aorto-enteric fistula: perigraft gas and loss of the graft–duodenum fat plane in a patient with a previous aortic graft and GI bleeding
  • Splenic artery aneurysm ruptures in pregnancy; pseudoaneurysm in pancreatitis bleeds
  • GI bleed: CTA arterial phase, no oral contrast; the blush changes shape on the delayed phase; tagged RBC for slow/intermittent bleeding
Never miss / red flags
  • Ruptured or symptomatic AAA
  • Acute aortic occlusion
  • Aorto-enteric fistula
  • SMA occlusion
  • Active GI haemorrhage with a visible blush
  • Visceral pseudoaneurysm
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Intraluminal hyperdense material from medication, ingested contrast or foreign bodies mimicking a blush — the non-contrast series answers it
  • Inflammatory aneurysm vs contained rupture
  • Retroperitoneal haematoma from another source (psoas, kidney) adjacent to an intact aneurysm
  • Mesenteric vasospasm in NOMI vs vasculitis

Pelvic & extremity arterial emergencies

📖 Requisites pp. 353–362FY + Board

Pelvis

Pelvic fracture haemorrhage: arterial-phase extravasation from the internal iliac branches (superior gluteal — the most common — internal pudendal, obturator, lateral sacral) treated by embolisation; venous plexus and cancellous bleeding managed by binder/packing; report haematoma volume and location (a haematoma >500 mL, or anterior to the sacrum with active blush, predicts the need for angiography). Iliac artery injury with pubic diastasis; iliac dissection or occlusion after catheterisation; ruptured iliac aneurysm (usually common iliac, >3.5 cm; presents with compression of the ureter, vein, or nerves, or rupture into the retroperitoneum/rectum). Post-partum haemorrhage and uterine AVM (embolisation of the uterine arteries); pelvic pseudoaneurysm after surgery; spontaneous rectus sheath and pelvic side-wall haematomas in anticoagulated patients (inferior epigastric artery).

Acute limb ischaemia

The six P's (pain, pallor, pulselessness, paraesthesia, paralysis, poikilothermia) and the Rutherford categories (I viable, IIa marginally threatened, IIb immediately threatened — motor loss, needs revascularisation within hours, III irreversible). Causes: embolism (AF, mural thrombus, aneurysm — lodges at bifurcations: femoral, popliteal trifurcation; an abrupt cut-off with a meniscus and few collaterals in an otherwise clean vessel), thrombosis of a pre-existing stenosis or bypass graft (diffuse atherosclerosis, well-developed collaterals), popliteal aneurysm thrombosis (>2 cm; bilateral in 50%, associated with AAA — thrombosis and distal embolisation "trash" the runoff), trauma (knee dislocation, supracondylar fracture, penetrating), dissection extending into the iliacs, popliteal entrapment and cystic adventitial disease in young patients, and iatrogenic injury (access site pseudoaneurysm — the "yin-yang" swirl with a to-and-fro neck on Doppler; AV fistula; dissection; retroperitoneal haematoma from a high femoral puncture). CTA runoff (aortic bifurcation to the feet, with a delayed pass for slow flow) shows the level and length of occlusion, the inflow and the runoff, collaterals, and the cause — the report should be surgical: "occlusion of the SFA from x to y, reconstitution of the popliteal above the knee via collaterals, three-vessel runoff." Blue toe syndrome (atheroembolism from an aortic plaque or aneurysm) and compartment syndrome after reperfusion (muscle swelling and hypoenhancement — a clinical diagnosis).

Upper limb

Subclavian and axillary artery injury with first rib/clavicle fractures and shoulder dislocation; thoracic outlet syndrome (arterial — post-stenotic subclavian aneurysm distal to a cervical rib with distal emboli; venous — Paget-Schroetter effort thrombosis of the subclavian vein in young athletes); brachial artery injury with supracondylar fractures (children) and elbow dislocations; radial/ulnar injury from penetrating hand trauma; hypothenar hammer syndrome (ulnar artery aneurysm/thrombosis in the hand from repetitive trauma — corkscrew ulnar artery and digital emboli); iatrogenic radial artery occlusion after catheterisation; embolism to the upper limb (less common than lower; usually cardiac).

Key points
  • Pelvic arterial blush → angiography; superior gluteal is the usual culprit
  • Embolus: abrupt meniscus cut-off, clean vessel, poor collaterals; thrombosis: diffuse disease, good collaterals
  • Popliteal aneurysm >2 cm: thrombosis and trash foot; look for the contralateral one and an AAA
  • Rutherford IIb needs revascularisation within hours — the CTA must not delay the surgeon
  • Report inflow, level/length of occlusion, reconstitution, and runoff
Never miss / red flags
  • Rutherford IIb/III ischaemia
  • Knee dislocation with popliteal injury
  • Ruptured iliac aneurysm
  • Access-site pseudoaneurysm with active bleeding or a retroperitoneal haematoma
  • Compartment syndrome
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Contrast timing too early in slow-flow limbs → false occlusion (delayed pass)
  • Calcified vessels obscuring the lumen on MIP — read the axial images
  • Vasospasm in a young trauma patient mimicking injury
  • Venous contamination mimicking an AV fistula

Venous emergencies

📖 Requisites pp. 362–368FY + Board

Neck and chest

Internal jugular vein injury (penetrating or iatrogenic — central line): a thrombosed or irregular IJV, perivascular haematoma, or extravasation; the venous phase is needed to see it. Septic thrombophlebitis of the IJV (Lemierre). Superior vena cava syndrome: obstruction of the SVC by tumour (lung cancer, lymphoma — 90%), fibrosing mediastinitis, or catheter/pacemaker-related thrombosis (an increasing proportion); CT shows the obstruction, a filling defect or encasement, and the collateral pathways (azygos/hemiazygos, chest wall, internal mammary, paravertebral), with the "hot quadrate lobe" sign (focal enhancement of segment IV from the recanalised paraumbilical vein). Central venous catheter complications: malposition (azygos, internal mammary, left superior intercostal, persistent left SVC — a line running down the left mediastinum, arterial placement — a line following the carotid/subclavian artery to the arch), pneumothorax, perforation with mediastinal/pleural infusion, fibrin sheath, and thrombosis. Pulmonary embolism is covered in the thorax chapter.

Abdomen

IVC thrombosis (extension of iliofemoral DVT, IVC filter thrombosis, hypercoagulable states, compression by tumour, congenital IVC anomalies), tumour thrombus (renal cell carcinoma, HCC, adrenal cortical carcinoma — an enhancing, expanding filling defect; report the cranial extent relative to the hepatic veins and right atrium), IVC filter complications (tilt, migration, strut penetration through the wall into the duodenum/aorta/psoas, fracture with embolised fragments, caval occlusion). Portal vein thrombosis (cirrhosis, malignancy, pancreatitis, pylephlebitis from appendicitis or diverticulitis — a filling defect in the portal vein with gas or an abscess in the liver; cavernous transformation in the chronic form), mesenteric venous thrombosis (thickened congested bowel, mesenteric oedema, ascites), Budd-Chiari (hepatic vein/IVC thrombosis, a mottled "nutmeg" liver with caudate sparing/hypertrophy), splenic vein thrombosis (gastric varices in pancreatitis), renal vein thrombosis, ovarian vein thrombosis (postpartum), and gonadal vein varicocele. May-Thurner syndrome: compression of the left common iliac vein by the right common iliac artery against the spine, causing left iliofemoral DVT in a young woman — treated with thrombolysis and a stent.

Extremities

Upper-extremity DVT: catheter/pacemaker-related (the majority), Paget-Schroetter effort thrombosis (subclavian vein at the thoracic outlet in young athletes), and malignancy; duplex ultrasound (non-compressibility, echogenic thrombus, absent phasic flow and absent augmentation, loss of respiratory variation as an indirect sign of central obstruction — the subclavian and brachiocephalic segments need CT/MR venography because they cannot be compressed). Lower-extremity DVT: compression ultrasound from the common femoral to the popliteal vein (two-point or whole-leg), with Doppler; a non-compressible vein is the diagnostic criterion; acute thrombus is hypoechoic and distends the vein; chronic thrombus is echogenic, retracted, with wall thickening and collaterals; calf-vein DVT is followed or treated; superficial venous thrombophlebitis within 3 cm of the saphenofemoral junction is treated like DVT. Phlegmasia cerulea dolens: massive iliofemoral thrombosis with venous outflow obstruction, arterial compromise, and limb-threat — thrombolysis/thrombectomy. Pelvic vein DVT (internal iliac, ovarian) is not seen on leg ultrasound: CT/MR venography when suspicion is high and the leg scan is negative. Indirect CT venography of the pelvis and legs after CTPA is used selectively. Post-thrombotic syndrome, venous ulcers and ruptured Baker cysts (the commonest DVT mimic — fluid tracking down the calf from a popliteal cyst) are the differentials.

Key points
  • SVC syndrome: malignancy 90%, catheters rising; hot quadrate lobe sign
  • IVC tumour thrombus enhances and expands the vein; state the cranial extent
  • Pylephlebitis: portal vein thrombus + liver abscess from an intra-abdominal source
  • Non-compressibility is the DVT criterion; the subclavian/iliac segments need CT/MR venography
  • May-Thurner: left leg DVT in a young woman; Paget-Schroetter: subclavian in a young athlete
  • Phlegmasia = limb threat
Never miss / red flags
  • SVC obstruction with airway or cerebral oedema
  • Phlegmasia cerulea dolens
  • Free-floating iliofemoral/IVC thrombus
  • Tumour thrombus reaching the right atrium
  • Arterial placement of a central line
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Mixing artefact in the IVC and iliac veins on the portal venous phase — delayed images
  • Bland vs tumour thrombus (enhancement, expansion)
  • Baker cyst, haematoma and cellulitis mimicking DVT
  • Chronic thrombus and webs vs acute

Lung scintigraphy for pulmonary embolism

Ventilation: Xe-133 gas or Tc-99m DTPA aerosol (or Technegas) before the perfusion study. Eight views (anterior, posterior, both laterals, four obliques). PE appears as a segmental or subsegmental wedge-shaped perfusion defect extending to the pleural surface with preserved ventilation (mismatch); matched defects and non-segmental defects suggest lung disease (COPD, pneumonia, effusion, atelectasis). Interpretation: PIOPED II modified criteria (high probability — ≥2 large mismatched segmental defects; low/very low — small defects, matched defects, non-segmental; normal) or, increasingly, the PISAPED/trinary reading of the perfusion-only scan with the chest radiograph (PE present / PE absent / non-diagnostic). A normal perfusion scan excludes clinically significant PE. Non-PE causes of mismatch: tumour compression, vasculitis, radiation, congenital vascular anomalies, and previous PE. The "stripe" sign (preserved peripheral perfusion at the pleural edge of a defect) argues against PE; the fissure sign; and the "hot spot" of clumped MAA from blood drawn into the syringe. Other uses: quantitative perfusion before lung resection, shunt quantification (MAA in the brain/kidneys = right-to-left shunt), and detection of PE recurrence on a baseline.

  • V/Q for CTPA contraindication, pregnancy and CTEPH

  • High-probability V/Q with haemodynamic compromise
  • Systemic MAA activity (right-to-left shunt) in a patient receiving IV particles
  • Clumped MAA from a syringe with blood — a technical artefact, not emboli
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Matched defects in COPD misread as PE
  • Hot spots from clumping
  • Effusions and atelectasis producing non-segmental defects
  • Reverse mismatch (ventilation defect with preserved perfusion) in mucus plugging/pneumonia

Brain death, infection, GI bleeding, hepatobiliary & scrotal scintigraphy

📖 Requisites pp. 379–394FY + Board

Brain death

Confirmatory test when the clinical examination is confounded (sedatives, hypothermia, metabolic). Tc-99m HMPAO or ECD (lipophilic, cross the intact blood–brain barrier) or, less ideally, DTPA/pertechnetate (flow study only). Flow phase: absent intracranial arterial flow with the "hot nose" sign (diverted external carotid flow to the nasal region). Static/SPECT: no cerebral or cerebellar uptake — the "empty light bulb". Pitfalls: a dural sinus can be seen with pertechnetate (venous filling via the scalp), decompressive craniectomy preserves some flow, and the test must be interpreted with the injection site checked for quality (a bolus of good quality is required).

Infection imaging

Labelled leukocytes: In-111 oxine (imaging at 18–24 h, normal distribution spleen > liver > marrow, no bowel or renal activity — best for abdominal infection and chronic osteomyelitis; 60 mL of the patient's blood, 2–3 hours of labelling, granulocytes) or Tc-99m HMPAO (imaging at 1–4 h; normal bowel and renal activity after 2–4 h limits abdominal evaluation; better resolution; used for acute osteomyelitis, extremities, IBD imaging at 1 h). Combined with Tc-99m sulfur colloid marrow imaging for prosthetic joints and the spine (infection = WBC uptake without corresponding marrow uptake — "incongruent"); the spine is a false-negative site for WBC scans (photopenic), so use gallium or FDG. Three-phase bone scan (flow, blood pool, delayed at 2–4 h): osteomyelitis is positive in all three phases; cellulitis is positive in flow and pool but not delayed; the diabetic foot and post-surgical bone need WBC/marrow correlation. Gallium-67: chronic infection, vertebral osteomyelitis, immunocompromised (PCP — diffuse lung uptake; lymphoma). Indications: fever of unknown origin, abdominal abscess (WBC or CT), inflammatory bowel disease activity (Tc-WBC at 1 h), musculoskeletal infection (osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, infected prosthesis, diabetic foot), and vascular graft infection (FDG-PET/WBC). Trauma and bone infarction: the bone scan detects occult fractures (positive within 24–72 h in most, delayed in the elderly), stress fractures (focal fusiform uptake vs the linear uptake of shin splints), sickle-cell bone infarction (photopenic acutely, then increased; distinguishing infarct from osteomyelitis needs the WBC or a combined marrow scan), and AVN of the femoral head (a "doughnut" of a cold centre with a hot rim).

Gastrointestinal bleeding and Meckel scan

Tc-99m labelled red blood cells (in vitro labelling — best labelling efficiency >95%): dynamic imaging for 60–90 minutes with delayed images up to 24 h; detects bleeding rates of 0.1 mL/min; the signs — a focus of activity that appears, increases, and moves through the bowel (antegrade and retrograde) — the movement is what distinguishes bleeding from fixed activity (vascular blush, varices, haemangioma, uterus, penile, free pertechnetate in the stomach/bladder). Sulfur colloid (rapid clearance by the liver/spleen gives high target-to-background but a short window of ~15 minutes). Localisation is approximate; CT angiography or catheter angiography follows a positive study for treatment. Meckel scan: Tc-99m pertechnetate (taken up by gastric mucosa, including the ectopic gastric mucosa in a Meckel diverticulum) — a focus in the right lower quadrant appearing at the same time as the stomach and increasing in intensity; premedication with cimetidine/ranitidine (blocks secretion, retains tracer), pentagastrin (increases uptake), and glucagon (reduces peristalsis) improves sensitivity (~85% in children). False positives: intussusception, duplication cysts with gastric mucosa, AVM, urinary tract activity, inflammatory bowel disease.

Hepatobiliary scintigraphy

Tc-99m iminodiacetic acid derivatives (mebrofenin — higher hepatic extraction, works at higher bilirubin; disofenin) are taken up by hepatocytes and excreted into bile without conjugation. Normal: liver at 5–10 minutes, biliary tree and gallbladder by 30–60 minutes, bowel by 60 minutes. Acute cholecystitis: non-visualisation of the gallbladder at 60 minutes and after morphine (0.04 mg/kg IV, which contracts the sphincter of Oddi — repeat imaging 30 minutes later) or on delayed 4-hour imaging; sensitivity ~95%, specificity ~90%. The "rim sign" (increased pericholecystic hepatic activity) indicates gangrenous cholecystitis. False positives: prolonged fasting (>24 h — sludge; give sincalide/CCK first) or a recent meal (<4 h — the gallbladder is contracted), severe hepatocellular dysfunction, TPN, alcoholism, and chronic cholecystitis with a thick wall. Acalculous cholecystitis in the ICU is a common indication. Biliary leak (activity outside the biliary tree/bowel — after cholecystectomy or trauma), biliary obstruction (good hepatic uptake, no bowel activity at 4–24 h; vs hepatocellular dysfunction — poor uptake and slow clearance), and the neonatal question of biliary atresia vs neonatal hepatitis: after phenobarbital priming for 5 days, any bowel activity at 24 h excludes atresia; no bowel activity with good uptake = atresia (surgery — Kasai before 60 days); poor hepatic uptake favours hepatitis. Chronic cholecystitis/biliary dyskinesia: gallbladder ejection fraction <35–38% after sincalide infused over 60 minutes.

Scrotal scintigraphy

Tc-99m pertechnetate flow and blood-pool images with a lead shield behind the scrotum: torsion — decreased or absent perfusion on the affected side (early), or a "halo/doughnut" of increased peritesticular activity with a photopenic centre in late/missed torsion (>24 h); epididymo-orchitis — increased flow and blood-pool activity; superseded by Doppler ultrasound but useful when ultrasound is equivocal or unavailable.

Key points
  • Brain death: no intracranial flow, hot nose, empty light bulb
  • In-111 WBC: 24 h, no bowel/renal activity; Tc-HMPAO WBC: 1–4 h, better resolution; spine is photopenic — use Ga/FDG; marrow scan for prostheses
  • Three-phase bone scan: osteomyelitis positive in all three; cellulitis in the first two
  • GI bleed: labelled RBC, 0.1 mL/min, activity that moves; Meckel: pertechnetate + cimetidine/pentagastrin/glucagon
  • HIDA: no gallbladder at 60 min + morphine or 4 h = acute cholecystitis; rim sign = gangrene; fasting/recent meal are the false positives; biliary atresia = no bowel at 24 h after phenobarbital
Never miss / red flags
  • Gangrenous cholecystitis (rim sign)
  • Bile leak after cholecystectomy
  • Active GI bleeding on the RBC scan → angiography
  • Missed torsion (halo sign) — the testis is lost; check the other side
Pitfalls & mimics
  • Fixed activity (renal pelvis, bladder, varices, uterus, penile blood pool) misread as GI bleeding
  • Free pertechnetate in the stomach/bladder on a Meckel scan
  • Non-visualised gallbladder from prolonged fasting/TPN
  • Duodenogastric reflux on HIDA misread as a leak
