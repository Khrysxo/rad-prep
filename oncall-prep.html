Thoracic Imaging

On-call thoracic radiology — covering life-threatening emergencies from aortic syndrome to pneumothorax. Emphasis on CT interpretation, protocol selection, and urgent communication.

CXRCTUrgentCritical Findings

🩸 Acute Aortic Syndrome

Definition: A spectrum of three closely related pathologies sharing similar clinical presentations:

  • Aortic Dissection — intimal tear → blood propagates within media → true and false lumens
  • Intramural Haematoma (IMH) — spontaneous bleeding of vasa vasorum into media (no intimal tear)
  • Penetrating Atherosclerotic Ulcer (PAU) — ulceration through intima into media; can progress to IMH/dissection/rupture

Aortic Wall Layers: Intima → Media → Adventitia. The dissection plane propagates within the media.

Demographics: Middle-aged to elderly; associations with hypertension, atherosclerosis. In younger patients consider Marfan syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome.

Classic Symptoms: Tearing chest/back pain, upper limb BP asymmetry. Note: absence does NOT exclude diagnosis.

🚨 Red Flags — Must NOT Miss

  • Type A dissection (ascending aorta) = surgical emergency — mortality rises by ~1-2% per hour untreated
  • Haemopericardium on CT → cardiac tamponade risk — check pericardial density (>25 HU = blood)
  • Involvement of coronary, carotid, or renal arteries signals end-organ ischaemia
  • Intramural haematoma can appear normal on enhanced CT — ALWAYS review unenhanced series first

📋 Imaging Protocol — Acute Aortic Syndrome

Phase 1Unenhanced — from just above aortic arch to diaphragm. Critical for IMH (crescenteric high attenuation in aortic wall).
Phase 2CTA (arterial): 100 ml IV contrast @ 4 ml/sec via 18G cannula. Bolus track on descending thoracic aorta. Scan: aortic arch → femoral heads.
WindowWidth 400 / Level 100 for aorta. Narrow window aids dissection flap identification.

CT Findings — Dissection

  • Dissection Flap Serpiginous linear filling defect dividing aortic lumen → true & false lumens
  • True Lumen Smaller, denser contrast, surrounded by intimal calcification; supplied by aortic root
  • False Lumen Larger, less dense, may become thrombosed over time
  • Cardiac Motion Artefact Linear defect in ascending aorta on non-ECG-gated study — normal, do NOT mistake for dissection flap
  • Haemopericardium >25 HU density in pericardial space = blood → tamponade risk

CT Findings — Intramural Haematoma

  • Unenhanced: crescenteric HIGH attenuation material within aortic wall (best on narrow window)
  • Enhanced: appears as LOW attenuation crescent (relative to IV contrast) — can look like atheroma
  • Key distinguishing feature: displaces intimal calcifications AWAY from aortic wall

CT Findings — Penetrating Aortic Ulcer

  • Focal outpouching/bulging of aortic wall, usually separating atherosclerotic calcification
  • Compare with prior imaging — new or enlarging ulcer is concerning
  • Can progress to IMH → dissection → rupture

💡 Key Points — Acute Aortic Syndrome

  • ALWAYS obtain unenhanced CT first — IMH is invisible on enhanced series alone
  • Window the aorta at W400/L100 — soft tissue windows only can give false negatives for dissection flap
  • Identify cranial and caudal extent of dissection — may need to extend scan to femoral heads
  • Check all major branches: brachiocephalic, carotids, celiac, SMA, IMA, renal arteries
  • Stanford classification has direct management implications — must be stated in every report

📝 Report Checklist

  • Presence or absence of intramural haematoma (unenhanced series)
  • Cranial and caudal extent of dissection flap
  • True vs. false lumen identification
  • Patency of: great vessels / celiac / SMA / IMA / renal arteries
  • Presence of pericardial blood / signs of cardiac tamponade
  • Stanford/DeBakey classification
  • Communicate urgently to referring team for Type A

💥 Thoracic Aortic Injury (Traumatic)

Spectrum from intramural haematoma → laceration → pseudoaneurysm → complete transection/rupture. Most common at aortic isthmus (region of tethering just distal to left subclavian artery).

Mortality: 80–90% without treatment. Must be identified urgently.

🚨 Classic CXR Signs of Aortic Injury

  • Mediastinal widening >8 cm (sensitivity 53–100%, specificity 1–60% — low specificity!)
  • Indistinct aortic contour
  • Left apical pleural cap
  • Tracheal deviation to right
  • Depression of left main bronchus below 40° to trachea
  • Note: CXR can be NORMAL in up to 7% of significant aortic injuries

CT Findings

  • Direct Signs Focal aortic contour deformity, pseudoaneurysm, mural discontinuity, active contrast extravasation
  • Periaortic Haematoma Increased attenuation >30 HU in mediastinum around aorta — extremely worrisome
  • Intraluminal Clot Filling defect within aortic lumen — compare with prior imaging to exclude atheroma
  • Aortic Isthmus Know normal appearance — pseudocoarctation can mimic injury

💡 Key Points

  • CT is gold standard for aortic trauma; unenhanced + CTA both required
  • Mediastinal haematoma most commonly from small mediastinal veins (not aorta) — don't panic immediately
  • Periaortic haematoma specifically is most worrisome for occult aortic injury
  • Any suspicion = urgent surgical/IR communication

🫁 Pulmonary Embolism

Medical emergency with variable clinical presentation. PE most commonly arises from DVT of lower extremities. Risk assessment via Wells Score is key clinical tool.

📊 Two-Level Wells Score

Clinical FeaturePoints
Signs/symptoms of DVT (leg swelling + deep vein pain)3

Immobilisation or surgery in previous 4 weeks1.5

Score ≤4: PE unlikely → D-dimer. Score >4: PE likely → CTPA immediately

📋 CTPA Protocol

Contrast80–100 ml IV @ 4–5 ml/sec via 18G antecubital vein (right arm preferred)
TriggerBolus track in main pulmonary artery (threshold 100 HU)
CoverageLung apices → adrenals (include pelvic veins if DVT query)
ReconstructionThin slices 1-1.5 mm; lung and mediastinal windows

CT Findings

  • Filling Defect Low attenuation intraluminal filling defect in pulmonary artery (acute PE is central, surrounded by contrast)
  • Railway Track Sign Contrast surrounds central clot in axial plane
  • Hampton's Hump Peripheral wedge-shaped opacity abutting pleura = pulmonary infarction
  • Westermark Sign CXR — oligaemia distal to occluded vessel
  • Fleischner Sign CXR — enlarged central pulmonary artery
  • RV Strain CT — RV:LV ratio >0.9 (axial), interventricular septal bowing toward LV, reflux of contrast into IVC
  • Mosaic Attenuation Heterogeneous lung attenuation on CT — reflects regional perfusion differences

🚨 Signs of Massive/Submassive PE on CT

  • Saddle embolus across main PA bifurcation
  • RV/LV ratio >1.0 — indicates right heart strain
  • Interventricular septal deviation toward LV
  • Reflux of contrast into IVC and hepatic veins
  • Right atrial thrombus
  • Flattening of IVC (sign of low venous return)

🔴 Acute PE

  • Central filling defect surrounded by contrast
  • Vessel distension at clot site
  • No vessel narrowing
  • May have pulmonary infarcts

🟡 Chronic PE

  • Eccentric/mural filling defect
  • Vessel narrowing/webs
  • Recanalization
  • Enlarged bronchial arteries

💡 Key Points — PE

  • D-dimer has near 100% NPV in low/intermediate probability — rules OUT PE when negative
  • D-dimer is NOT diagnostic — it is positive in many conditions (infection, malignancy, post-op)
  • CTPA is the gold standard imaging modality for PE diagnosis
  • V/Q scan preferred in renal impairment, iodine allergy, pregnancy (lower radiation)
  • Always check for right heart strain — influences management (thrombolysis vs. anticoagulation)
  • Incidental PE on staging CT — still requires management decision

💨 Pneumothorax

Air in the pleural space. Primary spontaneous (no underlying disease, tall thin males 20-40y); Secondary (COPD, asthma, CF, Marfan); Traumatic; Iatrogenic (post-procedure).

🚨 Tension Pneumothorax — Radiological Features

  • Large unilateral pneumothorax
  • Mediastinal shift AWAY from pneumothorax
  • Depression of ipsilateral hemidiaphragm
  • Compression of contralateral lung
  • ⚠️ Clinical diagnosis first — needle decompression before imaging if haemodynamically unstable

CXR Findings

  • Visceral Pleural Line Thin white line parallel to chest wall with no lung markings beyond it
  • Deep Sulcus Sign Supine CXR — deep lateral costophrenic recess (air collects anteriorly in supine position)
  • Size estimation: BTS guidelines use interpleural distance at hilum level (<2 cm = small; ≥2 cm = large)

CT Findings

  • Most sensitive — can detect tiny pneumothorax invisible on CXR
  • Identifies underlying lung disease (bullae in emphysema, metastases)
  • Occult pneumothorax: seen on trauma CT only, no clinical signs — monitor vs. drain debate

📝 Report Checklist

  • Size of pneumothorax (BTS criteria: <2 cm or ≥2 cm at hilum)
  • Signs of tension (mediastinal shift, hemidiaphragm depression)
  • Presence of underlying lung disease
  • Bilateral pneumothorax?
  • Previous chest tube position if present

Alveolar airspace filling pattern. Lobar consolidation = bacterial. Interstitial pattern = atypical/viral. Always assess for underlying cause, complications (empyema, abscess, ARDS).

🩸 AAA Rupture

Abdominal aortic aneurysm rupture is a surgical emergency. Defined as focal dilatation >50% of normal vessel diameter involving all three wall layers (intima, media, adventitia). Occurs in 2–4% of population >50 years. Mortality >65% before reaching hospital.

🚨 Clinical Emergency — Act Fast

  • Classic triad: hypotension + abdominal/back pain + pulsatile mass (often incomplete)
  • In elderly patient with abdominal pain + haemodynamic instability → AAA rupture until proven otherwise
  • CT immediately in unstable patients with strong clinical suspicion — do not delay for ultrasound
  • Communicate to surgical team WHILE still in scanner if rupture confirmed

📋 Imaging Protocol — AAA Rupture

PrimaryCT Aortic angiogram: 100 ml IV contrast via 18G cannula @ 4 ml/sec. Bolus track on mid-abdominal aorta. No oral contrast. Scan from diaphragm to femoral heads.
AlsoUnenhanced series for baseline (crescent sign, calcification). Both phases needed.
UltrasoundUseful in stable patients with unknown aortic history. Can confirm aneurysm but unreliable for rupture.

CT Findings

  • AAA confirmed Maximum aortic diameter >3 cm
  • Retroperitoneal Haematoma Most common; periaortic/psoas/pararenal haematoma = confirmed rupture
  • Intraperitoneal Rupture Free fluid in peritoneal cavity — less common but more lethal
  • Active Extravasation High-attenuation blush on arterial phase accumulating on venous phase
  • Draped Aorta Sign Posterior aortic wall inseparable from vertebral body → contained rupture
  • Hyperattenuating Crescent High attenuation material within thrombus sac = blood infiltration into thrombus → impending rupture
  • Aortoenteric Fistula Gas within aortic lumen; contrast extending into bowel lumen (usually 2nd/3rd duodenum)

Morphology Clues

  • Fusiform: Degenerative (most common) — involves all walls uniformly
  • Saccular/lobulated: Suspect mycotic aneurysm — also look for periaortic inflammation, vertebral body destruction, fluid collections
  • Mycotic pathogens: Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Salmonella (East Asia — early rupture tendency)

Endovascular vs. Open Repair Assessment

  • Infrarenal vs. suprarenal: infrarenal preferred for EVAR
  • Measure distance from renal arteries to aneurysm neck (landing zone needed)
  • Check iliac artery calibre (>8 mm) and tortuosity for EVAR eligibility
  • Angulated or narrow iliac arteries → open surgical repair favoured

💡 Key Points

  • CT is gold standard — both unenhanced and arterial phases required
  • AAA confirmed when max aortic diameter >3 cm
  • Rupture confirmed in presence of retroperitoneal/periaortic haematoma
  • Hyperattenuating crescent sign + rapid growth (>10 mm/yr) + pain = impending rupture
  • Check IVC: if flat/slit-like = significant intravascular volume depletion

🔴 Acute GI Bleeding

Mortality up to 40%. Upper GI bleeding (>ligament of Treitz) = 75% of cases. Lower GI = 25%. Endoscopy is first-line but CT increasingly used as adjunct.

📋 CTA Protocol for GI Bleeding (Triple Phase)

Phase 1Unenhanced — no oral contrast. Identify pre-existing high-attenuation within bowel (tablets, foreign body, prior barium).
Phase 2Arterial: 100 ml IV @ 4 ml/sec, bolus track mid-abdominal aorta. Scan diaphragm to femoral heads.
Phase 3Delayed (120 sec): accumulation of contrast confirms active bleeding vs. vascular blush

CT Findings

  • Active Extravasation >90 HU focal hyperattenuation within bowel lumen on arterial phase → accumulates on delayed phase
  • Confirm on Delayed True bleed accumulates/increases; contrast blush (pseudoaneurysm) may decrease
  • Oesophageal Varices Dilated serpiginous vessels in distal oesophagus → look for portal hypertension signs
  • Portal HTN Signs Splenomegaly, ascites, varices (splenic/oesophageal), irregular liver outline, para-umbilical vein enhancement

🚨 False Positives to Exclude

  • Ingested tablets (aspirin, iron, bismuth — high density on unenhanced)
  • Residual oral contrast or barium (especially in diverticula — can persist years!)
  • Cone-beam artefact at air-fluid interfaces within bowel
  • Review unenhanced series to identify pre-existing densities

💡 Key Points

  • Triple-phase CTA (unenhanced + arterial + delayed) maximises sensitivity/specificity
  • Active bleed = >90 HU focus in bowel lumen that increases on delayed phase
  • Always scrutinise unenhanced phase to exclude false positives
  • Catheter angiography reserved for post-CTA embolisation — less sensitive than CTA
  • GI bleeding is often intermittent — normal scan does NOT exclude bleeding
  • Report the vessel involved and the main artery of which it is a branch

💨 Bowel Perforation

GI tract wall breach is a surgical emergency. Causes: peptic ulcer disease, inflammatory bowel disease, trauma, iatrogenic, foreign body, neoplasm. CT has 86% sensitivity for perforation site.

Plain Film Signs

  • Free Air under Diaphragm Erect CXR — as little as 1 ml can be detected. Do NOT confuse stomach bubble under left hemidiaphragm.
  • Rigler's Sign Gas outlining BOTH sides of bowel wall on supine AXR
  • Football Sign Oval-shaped peritoneal gas (more common in children)
  • Falciform Ligament Sign Gas outlining the falciform ligament
  • Triangle Sign Gas between three loops of bowel
  • Lucency over RUQ Gas anterior to liver (supine film)
  • Left lateral decubitus: detects small air between liver and lateral peritoneal wall
  • Plain films only 50–70% sensitive — CT required for further assessment

CT Findings & Localisation

  • Free air: small locules near liver edge, peritoneum, or large collections — use LUNG WINDOWS to find subtle free air
  • Supramesocolic gas (above transverse colon) → upper GI perforation (stomach, duodenal bulb)
  • Inframesocolic gas → distal small bowel / large bowel perforation
  • Retroperitoneal gas → 2nd/3rd duodenum, ascending/descending colon perforation
  • Look for: wall defect, inflammatory stranding, localised abscess, phlegmon

⚠️ Iatrogenic Gas — Don't Call Perforation

  • Post-operative free air: expected up to day 10, should be <10 ml; large volumes after day 10 = concern for anastomotic leak
  • Other sources: laparoscopy, peritoneal dialysis, vaginal fistula, G-tube placement
  • Review clinical history BEFORE calling perforation

🌀 Bowel Ischaemia & Enterocolitis

Interruption of bowel blood supply → transmural infarction if not treated. Causes: SMA/SMV thrombosis, non-occlusive (low-flow state), strangulation, volvulus.

CT Findings (Spectrum of Severity)

  • Bowel Wall Thickening >3 mm — earliest sign; can be mural oedema (non-specific)
  • Hypoenhancement Pale/non-enhancing bowel wall on IV contrast = transmural ischaemia — urgent!
  • Pneumatosis Intestinalis Gas within bowel wall — submucosal (bubbly) or subserosal (linear, MORE ominous = necrosis)
  • Portal/Mesenteric Venous Gas Gas in portal veins or mesenteric veins = severe ischaemia / necrosis
  • SMA/SMV Thrombus Filling defect in mesenteric vessels — can be subtly low density
  • Free Fluid/Ascites Non-specific but present in advanced ischaemia
  • Target Sign Alternating enhancing/non-enhancing rings = mural oedema (non-specific)

🚨 Pneumatosis — When to Panic

  • Linear pattern in context of ischaemia = transmural bowel necrosis = surgical emergency
  • Bubbly/cystic pattern (pneumatosis cystoides) can be benign (steroids, COPD, bowel obstruction)
  • Portal venous gas + pneumatosis = almost always indicates irreversible bowel necrosis
  • Portal venous gas alone has many causes (benign: after colonoscopy, in liver disease)

Arterial Ischaemia (SMA)

  • Sudden onset; often AF or atherosclerosis
  • Small bowel predominantly
  • SMA filling defect on CTA
  • Rapid transmural infarction

Venous Ischaemia (SMV)

  • More gradual onset; hypercoagulable states
  • SMV/portal thrombus on CTA
  • Mesenteric oedema, ascites prominent
  • Better prognosis than arterial

🔴 Acute Appendicitis

Most common cause of acute abdomen requiring surgery. Obstruction of appendiceal lumen → bacterial overgrowth → inflammation → perforation. CT is primary imaging modality in adults.

CT Criteria for Appendicitis

  • Appendix Diameter >6 mm (outer wall to outer wall) + periappendiceal fat stranding = appendicitis
  • Periappendiceal Stranding Inflammatory fat stranding around appendix — essential finding
  • Wall Thickening Appendiceal wall >2 mm thick, enhancing on IV contrast
  • Appendicolith Calcified stone in appendix lumen → increased perforation risk
  • Perforation Signs Extraluminal gas, periappendiceal abscess, phlegmon, free fluid
  • Arrowhead Sign Focal thickening of caecal tip pointing toward appendix

Ultrasound Criteria

  • Non-compressible appendix >6 mm diameter
  • Hyperechoic periappendiceal fat
  • Appendicolith (echogenic focus with shadowing)
  • Sensitivity ~86%, Specificity ~81% — limited by operator, body habitus, gas, deep pelvis
  • First-line in children and pregnant women (no radiation)

⚠️ Pitfalls & Mimics

  • Mesenteric adenitis (children): multiple enlarged lymph nodes, normal appendix
  • Epiploic appendagitis: fat-density oval lesion with hyperattenuating rim adjacent to colon; no treatment needed
  • Omental infarction: large fat-density mass, pericolonic stranding, no appendix involvement
  • Crohn's disease: terminal ileitis may mimic; look for skip lesions, fat wrapping, fistulae
  • Ovarian pathology in women: always check adnexa

💡 Key Points

  • Appendix >6 mm + fat stranding = appendicitis on CT until proven otherwise
  • Report: perforation? periappendiceal abscess? free fluid? appendicolith?
  • MRI: preferred in pregnancy (no radiation, high sensitivity)
  • Appendix not visualised on ultrasound in up to 30% of adults — proceed to CT if clinical suspicion

Neurology & Non-Traumatic Spinal Imaging

Stroke, haemorrhage, SAH, venous sinus thrombosis, meningitis, carotid dissection, and spinal cord emergencies. Time-critical imaging for thrombolysis decisions.

Time CriticalCT/MRIThrombolysis

⚡ Stroke — Ischaemic & Haemorrhagic

Ischaemia = 80% of strokes. Haemorrhage = 20%. Cannot be distinguished clinically — imaging is essential. Thrombolysis window: <4.5 hours from symptom onset (some centres up to 9 hours with perfusion imaging).

🚨 ALWAYS Exclude Haemorrhage First on CT

  • Haemorrhage = absolute contraindication to thrombolysis
  • Unenhanced CT is fast and excellent at identifying acute blood
  • Normal CT does NOT exclude acute ischaemia — MRI DWI is more sensitive in first 6 hours

CT Findings — Acute Ischaemic Stroke

  • Hyperdense MCA Sign Hyperdense thrombus in MCA or its branches — acute thrombus
  • Insular Ribbon Sign Loss of grey-white matter differentiation of insular cortex — early cytotoxic oedema (window: W8/L32)
  • Loss of Basal Ganglia Subtle blurring of caudate head / lentiform nucleus outline
  • Cortical Sulci Effacement Early oedema causing sulcal filling
  • Wedge-shaped low attenuation following vascular territory = established infarction
  • Chronic: encephalomalacia (CSF density), ex-vacuo ventricular dilation

CT Findings — Haemorrhagic Stroke

  • Hypertensive haemorrhage: basal ganglia, thalamus, brainstem, cerebellum (deep locations)
  • Lobar haemorrhage (atypical) → consider: AVM, cavernoma, venous thrombosis, amyloid angiopathy, underlying tumour
  • Document: size, location, mass effect, midline shift, ventricular extension, herniation
  • Herniation on CT: midline shift, effacement of basal cisterns, tonsillar descent below foramen magnum

💥 Subarachnoid Haemorrhage (SAH)

Thunderclap headache — worst headache of life. 85% from ruptured aneurysm. Time-critical: 30-day mortality 45% without treatment.

CT Findings

  • Hyperdense blood filling subarachnoid cisterns, sulci, fissures
  • Basal cisterns first: interpeduncular, sylvian fissure, interhemispheric fissure
  • Star Sign Blood in all cisterns around midbrain
  • CT sensitivity: >98% within 6 hours; drops to 85–90% at 24 hours
  • If CT negative + high clinical suspicion → LP (xanthochromia 2–12h after onset)
  • Complications: hydrocephalus, vasospasm, re-bleed

Aneurysmal SAH Distribution

  • ACA/ACom → interhemispheric + bifrontal
  • MCA → sylvian fissure (unilateral)
  • PCom/basilar → basal cisterns + posterior fossa
  • Perimesencephalic → around midbrain (nonaneurysmal, good prognosis)

Next Steps After SAH Diagnosis

  • CT Angiography immediately to find aneurysm
  • Neurosurgical/neurointerventional referral
  • Nimodipine for vasospasm prophylaxis
  • Monitor for hydrocephalus (repeat CT)

🌊 Venous Sinus Thrombosis (CVST)

Thrombosis of dural venous sinuses → venous hypertension → haemorrhagic infarction. Female > Male. Associated with OCP, pregnancy, dehydration, hypercoagulable states.

Acute Conditions of Male Reproductive Organs

Emergency Radiology Ch.8 — Testicular torsion, epididymo-orchitis, Fournier's gangrene.

♂️ Testicular Torsion — Time Critical

Salvage rate: >90% if operated within 6h; <10% after 24h. Twisting of spermatic cord → venous outflow obstruction → ischaemia. Peak: adolescent males.

Ultrasound Findings

  • Absent/decreased Doppler flow in affected testis (compare with contralateral)
  • Twisted spermatic cord ("whirlpool sign")
  • Testicular heterogeneity (late sign = necrosis)
  • Reactive hydrocele
  • High-riding testis with abnormal orientation

🚨 Torsion vs. Epididymo-Orchitis

  • Torsion: Absent flow, acute onset, young male; surgical emergency
  • Epididymo-orchitis: INCREASED flow in epididymis/testis; gradual onset; STI or urinary pathogen; antibiotics

ER10

Acute Nontraumatic Liver & Spleen

Emergency Radiology Ch.10 — Hepatic abscess, Budd-Chiari, hepatic infarction, splenic infarction, spontaneous haemoperitoneum.

🫀 Budd-Chiari Syndrome

Hepatic venous outflow obstruction. Acute: RUQ pain, ascites, hepatomegaly. Chronic: cirrhosis pattern.

  • CT: central liver enhancement with peripheral non-enhancement (centripetal enhancement pattern)
  • Caudate lobe hypertrophy (drains directly to IVC — preserved)
  • Thrombus in hepatic veins on CT/US Doppler
  • Comma-shaped intrahepatic collaterals

Pyogenic Liver Abscess

  • CT: rim-enhancing hypodense collection; often multilocular; pericholecystic stranding
  • Clusters sign: multiple small abscesses clustering
  • Treatment: IR percutaneous drainage + IV antibiotics

Splenic Infarction

  • CT: wedge-shaped peripheral non-enhancing area in spleen
  • Causes: emboli (AF), haematological conditions (sickle cell, lymphoma), vasculitis
  • Global infarction (entire spleen) = surgical/IR emergency

ER11

Imaging of Acute Pancreas

Emergency Radiology Ch.11 — Pancreatitis grading, necrotizing pancreatitis, complications.

🔬 Necrotizing Pancreatitis

Non-enhancing pancreatic parenchyma = necrosis. >30% necrosis = significant. Balthazar CTSI predicts morbidity/mortality.

💡 Revised Atlanta Classification (2012)

  • Acute peripancreatic fluid collection (APFC): Homogeneous fluid, no wall; <4 weeks; sterile
  • Pancreatic pseudocyst: Well-defined wall, no necrosis; >4 weeks; mostly fluid content
  • Acute necrotic collection (ANC): Mixed density, no wall; <4 weeks; solid + liquid
  • Walled-off necrosis (WON): Well-defined wall; >4 weeks; solid + liquid; may need drainage

🚨 Vascular Complications of Pancreatitis

  • Splenic artery pseudoaneurysm (most common) — risk of catastrophic haemorrhage
  • Splenic vein thrombosis → sinistral (left-sided) portal hypertension → gastric varices
  • SMA/celiac pseudoaneurysm
  • All require urgent angiography/embolisation

🤰 Ectopic Pregnancy

Implantation outside uterine cavity. Tubal ectopic = 95%. Life-threatening if ruptured. β-hCG + US = primary workup.

Ultrasound Findings

  • Empty uterus (no IUP) — discriminatory zone: IUP should be visible on TVUS when β-hCG >1500 IU/L

  • Placenta overlying internal os
  • US: transvaginal (safe); measure OS-placental distance

ER13

Acute Gynecologic Disorders

Emergency Radiology Ch.13 — Ovarian torsion, tubo-ovarian abscess, leiomyoma complications.

♀️ Ovarian Torsion

Twisting of ovarian pedicle → venous obstruction → oedema → infarction. Any age; enlarged ovary (>5 cm) or mass increases risk.

  • Enlarged, heterogeneous ovary with peripheral cysts (follicles pushed to periphery)
  • Absent/decreased Doppler flow (absent flow in >60%; present flow does NOT exclude torsion)
  • Free fluid
  • Whirlpool Sign Twisted ovarian pedicle on US/CT
  • CT: free fluid, enlarged ovary, whirlpool of twisted vessels

🚨 Key Point

Presence of Doppler flow does NOT exclude ovarian torsion — intermittent torsion can have preserved flow. Clinical suspicion → direct to gynae for surgical exploration.

Tubo-ovarian Abscess (TOA)

  • Complex adnexal mass with thick irregular wall + internal echoes ± free fluid
  • CT: rim-enhancing complex collection in adnexa; associated free fluid
  • Treatment: IV antibiotics ± IR drainage

ASPECTS Score (0–10)

Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score. Assesses 10 regions in MCA territory. Score of 10 = no early ischaemic changes; score <6 = poor outcome; thrombectomy typically for ASPECTS ≥6.

💡 CTP Maps — What to Look For

  • CBV (cerebral blood volume): Core infarct = reduced CBV (dead tissue)
  • CBF (cerebral blood flow): Penumbra = reduced CBF but preserved CBV
  • MTT (mean transit time): Prolonged = ischaemia
  • Tmax: >6s = penumbra (salvageable); >10s = severe ischaemia
  • Target mismatch: penumbra:core ratio >1.8 → thrombectomy benefit (DAWN/DEFUSE3)

💪 Upper Extremity Fractures — High Yield

Shoulder

  • Anterior dislocation (90%): Hill-Sachs lesion (posterolateral humeral head defect); Bankart lesion (anteroinferior glenoid fracture)

🦵 Lower Extremity — High Yield Board Topics

Hip Fractures (Garden Classification)

  • Garden I: incomplete/impacted (valgus) — IV → conservative
  • Garden II: complete, undisplaced — arthroplasty
  • Garden III: complete, partial displacement
  • Garden IV: complete, full displacement → highest AVN risk → arthroplasty

Lisfranc Injury (Tarsometatarsal)

  • Often missed on initial XR! — look for 1st/2nd metatarsal malalignment
  • Normal: medial border 2nd MT aligns with medial border middle cuneiform
  • Fleck sign: avulsion fragment between 1st and 2nd MT base = Lisfranc ligament avulsion
  • Weight-bearing XR or CT if suspected on non-weight-bearing XR

Ottawa Ankle Rules

  • XR indicated if: pain in malleolar zone + bony tenderness at posterior tip of either malleolus (6 cm) OR inability to weight-bear
  • Foot XR if: bony tenderness at 5th MT base OR at navicular + inability to weight-bear

ER24

Imaging of Acute Thoracic Trauma

Emergency Radiology Ch.24 — Pneumothorax, haemothorax, rib fractures, sternal and clavicular injuries, cardiac contusion.

💨 Thoracic Trauma — Systematic Review

Rib Fractures

  • Ribs 1–2: high energy → associated aortic/great vessel injury
  • Ribs 3–9: most common; look for pneumothorax, haemothorax, pulmonary contusion
  • Ribs 10–12: lower → splenic/hepatic injury
  • Flail chest: ≥3 consecutive ribs fractured in ≥2 places → paradoxical breathing → respiratory failure

Pulmonary Contusion

  • GGO/consolidation appearing within 6h of trauma; resolves 3–5 days
  • Does NOT follow segmental distribution (unlike aspiration/pneumonia)
  • Located adjacent to chest wall impact site or contrecoup

Pneumomediastinum

  • Gas tracking in mediastinal planes → neck, chest wall
  • Causes: oesophageal rupture, tracheal/bronchial tear, pneumothorax tracking, alveolar rupture
  • Oesophageal rupture = most lethal → look for left pleural effusion + mediastinal gas
  • Continuous Diaphragm Sign Gas between pericardium and diaphragm = pneumomediastinum on CXR
  • Naclerio's V Sign Gas outlining left paramediastinal-diaphragmatic triangle = oesophageal rupture

🩸 CT Aorta — Suspected Dissection/Rupture

Protocol

Phase 1Unenhanced from thoracic inlet to femoral heads (no oral contrast)
Phase 2Arterial CTA: 100 ml IV @ 4 ml/sec via 18G antecubital cannula. Bolus track on descending aorta (trigger 100 HU). Scan thoracic inlet → femoral heads.
WindowsAortic: W400/L100. Soft tissue: W400/L40. Report both.
ReconstructionAxial + coronal + sagittal + curved planar reformats. 1 mm slices.

🫁 CTPA — Pulmonary Embolism

Protocol

Contrast80–100 ml IV @ 4–5 ml/sec. 18G right antecubital vein preferred.
TriggerBolus track at main pulmonary artery (100 HU threshold)
CoverageLung apices to adrenals. Extend to thighs/popliteal if DVT suspected.
Slices1–1.5 mm axial. Lung windows essential for PE interpretation.
AssessRight heart strain: RV:LV ratio on axial; interventricular septum; IVC reflux.

🔴 CT Abdomen/Pelvis — Acute Pain

Standard Portal Venous Phase Protocol

IndicationAcute abdomen; appendicitis query; bowel obstruction
Contrast100 ml IV @ 3 ml/sec. Portal venous phase 60–70 seconds delay.
Oral contrastNot required in emergency (delays scan; reduces sensitivity for pathology)
CoverageDiaphragm to pubic symphysis
ReconstructionAxial + coronal 2 mm. Lung window preset for free air detection.

Triple Phase (GI Bleeding / Vascular)

Phase 1Unenhanced: identify pre-existing high attenuation (tablets, barium, blood)
Phase 2Arterial (25–30 sec): active bleeding, pseudoaneurysm
Phase 3Delayed (120 sec): confirms active extravasation vs. artefact

⚡ CT Brain — Acute Stroke

Acute Stroke Protocol (if <4.5h)

Step 1Non-contrast CT head — exclude haemorrhage (must be done first)
Step 2CTA head + neck — identify LVO. 100 ml @ 5 ml/sec, aortic arch to vertex.
Step 3CT Perfusion — if considering extended window or uncertain penumbra. Dynamic acquisition through brain after 50 ml IV.
ReportASPECTS score, LVO level, any haemorrhage, RV strain (if PE also queried)
